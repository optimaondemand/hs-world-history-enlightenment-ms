The Coup of 18 Brumaire
Lesson · From Revolution to Empire, Part II of II
💡 GLOSSARY OF SUBJECT VOCABULARY
A few words worth reviewing before you read.
coup d'état (koo day‑TAH): the sudden, often extralegal overthrow of a government, usually by a small group already holding some power within it.
Council of Five Hundred: the lower, larger legislative chamber under the Directory, whose members resisted Napoleon's takeover most fiercely.
Council of Ancients: the upper, smaller legislative chamber under the Directory, whose cooperation made the coup possible in the first place.
outlaw (as a verb, in this context): to formally declare someone outside the law's protection, historically a step toward summary execution.
📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
Was Napoleon the Revolution's heir or its undoing?
On November 9 and 10, 1799, by the revolutionary calendar's 18th and 19th of Brumaire, Napoleon and a small circle of co-conspirators overthrew the Directory in a chaotic, nearly failed operation. What makes this coup worth studying closely is not only what happened, but how Napoleon himself chose to describe it to the French public the very same night, in the primary source you are about to read.

📚 PART 1 · A COUP THAT NEARLY FAILED
⚡ QUICK CHECK-IN
If the person who seized power is also the only detailed source describing exactly how they seized it, how much should you trust their account? (No wrong answer.)

Two Councils, One Chaotic Afternoon

The coup's first stage went smoothly. On 18 Brumaire (November 9, 1799), sympathetic members of the Directory and the more cooperative Council of Ancients voted to relocate France's legislative bodies to the palace of Saint-Cloud, officially for security, and placed troops under Napoleon's command to protect the move. This gave the conspirators physical control of the setting where the next day's real confrontation would happen.

The second stage, on 19 Brumaire, nearly collapsed. The larger, more radical Council of Five Hundred reacted with fury once they realized the relocation was cover for a power grab, and by several accounts, including Napoleon's own, he was physically jostled and threatened by armed deputies when he entered their chamber uninvited. Napoleon's own soldiers, not persuasive argument, ultimately cleared the hostile council by force, after which a much smaller, cooperative remnant of deputies voted through the emergency measures the conspirators wanted, including the creation of the three-Consul government with Napoleon at its head.

That same night, Napoleon issued a public proclamation explaining the coup to the French people. It is worth reading his own version closely and skeptically at the same time, since it is, quite plainly, an account written by the person with the strongest possible interest in how the story gets told.

📚 PART 2 · READING A SELF-INTERESTED SOURCE CAREFULLY

Napoleon's proclamation, which you will read in full in Primary Source 4.16, casts him as a reluctant hero: a man who “refused to be the man of a party,” who entered the hostile council chamber “alone, unarmed,” and who acted only out of duty when assassins allegedly menaced him with daggers. Notice what work each of these details does. A reluctant, unarmed, nearly-murdered defender of liberty is a far more sympathetic figure than a general who used loyal troops to physically expel elected representatives who opposed him, which is closer to what most historians conclude actually happened.

This does not mean the proclamation is simply a lie from beginning to end. Contemporary accounts do generally agree there was real chaos and real danger in the Council of Five Hundred's chamber that day; France's political factions genuinely had reason to fear one another after a decade of revolutionary violence. The proclamation's most useful lesson for a careful historian is not whether it is true or false as a whole, but how a participant with everything to gain from public opinion chose to shape a genuinely chaotic, ambiguous event into a clean, flattering narrative.

The coup's aftermath followed a pattern worth noticing in its own right. Within weeks, the new government drafted the Constitution of the Year VIII, concentrating real executive power in Napoleon as First Consul while leaving the other two consuls with largely advisory roles. Rather than simply imposing this new constitution, Napoleon submitted it to a national plebiscite, a direct popular vote, which approved it by an overwhelming and suspiciously lopsided margin. Historians have long debated how freely that vote was actually conducted, but the choice to hold a plebiscite at all shows Napoleon's consistent instinct: seize power decisively, then dress the seizure in the language of popular consent the Revolution itself had made impossible to ignore.

That gap between the coup's messy reality and its polished retelling is itself part of the answer to this week's central question. A revolution that began by insisting the people's representatives alone held legitimate authority ended this particular chapter with soldiers deciding, at gunpoint in all but name, which representatives got to keep speaking.

💡 A CLOSER LOOK AT THE HISTORY
Nothing about the coup's success was guaranteed in advance. Napoleon's own brother Lucien, presiding over the Council of Five Hundred that day, played a decisive and largely improvised role in preventing the council from formally declaring Napoleon an outlaw once the chaos began, reportedly drawing a sword and threatening to stab his own brother rather than see him voted a traitor, a piece of family loyalty and quick thinking historians treat as genuinely close to changing the entire outcome.

💪 GOVERNING YOUR OWN JUDGMENT OF A SELF-SERVING STORY

Self-government includes governing what you believe, especially when a powerful, persuasive voice hands you a tidy story about their own conduct. Reading Napoleon's proclamation well does not mean rejecting it outright, or accepting it at face value. It means doing the harder work of weighing it against what else you know, exactly the discipline this course has been asking of you all quarter.
