💡 GLOSSARY OF SUBJECT VOCABULARY
Carried over from Lesson 2.3, plus a few new terms.
natural rights: rights that belong to people simply by virtue of being human, not granted by government.
consent: agreement, especially the agreement of the governed that Locke says legitimate government requires.
tacit (TAS‑it): implied or understood without being directly stated.
social contract: an agreement, real or hypothetical, by which people give up some freedom to a government in exchange for order and protection.
📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
Is government a contract a people may dissolve?
Hobbes looked at the state of nature and saw a war everyone should be desperate to escape, at almost any cost. John Locke, writing a generation later, looked at the same starting point and reached a strikingly different conclusion: that people carry real rights into political society, and that a government which violates those rights too badly can justly be resisted.

Rights That Exist Before Government

John Locke lived through the same turbulent seventeenth-century England as Hobbes, including years of his own political exile, but he published his major political work, Two Treatises of Government, in 1689, right after the Glorious Revolution replaced King James II with William and Mary in a relatively bloodless transfer of power. That different ending shaped a different theory. Where Hobbes imagined the state of nature as inevitable war, Locke imagined it as a condition of natural freedom and rough equality, governed loosely by a law of reason: people generally ought not to harm one another's life, health, liberty, or property, even without a government to enforce that rule.

Locke's own path to this position was not purely theoretical. He served as a physician and advisor to Anthony Ashley Cooper, Earl of Shaftesbury, a leading opponent of King James II's policies, and when Shaftesbury's faction fell from royal favor, Locke fled to the Dutch Republic around 1683, living there in political exile until the Glorious Revolution made it safe to return home. He drafted much of the Two Treatises during those years abroad, in the same Dutch Republic that had sheltered Descartes' philosophy roughly half a century earlier.

Locke also argued that individuals acquire rightful property by mixing their own labor with what nature provides: a person who clears a field or gathers fruit makes that labor, and its product, rightfully theirs. This labor theory of property gave Locke's natural rights a concrete economic dimension Hobbes's argument never developed, and it later shaped English and American arguments about ownership and land, including, controversially, arguments used to justify seizing land from peoples who used it differently than European farmers did.

Locke did not deny that the state of nature could turn violent. He agreed people would eventually need government to judge disputes fairly and punish wrongdoers reliably, since in the state of nature everyone is left to judge and enforce their own rights, which invites bias and escalation. But Locke's starting point was gentler than Hobbes's, and that difference in starting point drives everything that follows.

📚 PART 2 · WHEN TRUST IS FORFEITED

Because Locke believed people carry natural rights into political society rather than surrendering them entirely, he concluded that government exists to protect those rights, chiefly life, liberty, and property, by the consent of the governed, not to override them. If a government seriously and persistently violates the very rights it was created to protect, Locke argued, it breaks the trust the people placed in it, and political power reverts to the people, who may then resist, replace, or reconstitute their government.

Locke was careful to distinguish two different kinds of collapse. The dissolution of society itself, he wrote, usually happens only through outside conquest, when a foreign power simply destroys a political community by force. The dissolution of a particular government, by contrast, can happen from within, most importantly when those in power start ruling by their own arbitrary will instead of by laws the society agreed to. When that happens, Locke argued, the government has effectively dissolved itself, even if the people in office have not physically left.

This is a genuinely radical claim for its time: that ordinary people retain, permanently, the standing to judge whether their own government has broken faith with them, and to act on that judgment. Locke was not calling for constant revolution. He believed people tolerate a great deal before resisting. But he insisted the right to resist real tyranny never disappears simply because a government currently holds power.

Locke's timing mattered. Much of the argument was likely drafted years earlier, during a tense political crisis over royal succession, but Two Treatises was published just after the Glorious Revolution of 1688, and readers at the time received it as a defense of that recent, still-controversial event. James II's own subjects had judged that he ruled arbitrarily, invited William of Orange to take the throne instead, and replaced him with comparatively little bloodshed. Locke's theory gave that transfer of power a philosophical justification, arguing the English people had exercised a right that had been theirs all along, not committed treason against a rightful king.

⚡ TRY IT · HOBBES OR LOCKE?
For each claim below, decide whether it sounds more like Hobbes or more like Locke.
1. “Even a harsh government is better than the war that would otherwise consume us.”
2. “People do not give up their basic rights just by agreeing to live under a government.”
3. “The sovereign cannot break the social contract, because the sovereign was never a party to it.”
4. “When rulers act by their own arbitrary will instead of by law, they dissolve the government themselves.”
⚠️ COMMON MISTAKE
Do not read Locke as an anarchist, and do not read Hobbes as someone who thought tyranny was good. Locke still believed government was necessary and valuable; he simply set firmer limits on it. Hobbes still believed a sovereign could rule badly; he simply thought even bad order beat no order at all. Both men were trying to solve the same problem. They disagreed about which risk was worse.
HISTORICAL FIGURE
John Locke
LOK · 1632–1704
Locke was an English philosopher and physician whose 1689 Two Treatises of Government argued that people carry natural rights into political society and may justly resist a government that persistently violates them. Written around the time of England's Glorious Revolution, his ideas on natural rights, consent, and the right of resistance would echo directly in the American Declaration of Independence nearly a century later.

💪 GOVERNMENT AS A STANDING TRUST

Locke's argument asks something demanding of ordinary citizens: not blind obedience, and not constant suspicion, but an ongoing, sober judgment about whether power is still being used for the purpose it was granted. That is its own kind of self-government, exercised collectively rather than alone, and it is the thread that will run straight through next week's lessons and directly into the American Revolution in a few weeks, when a group of colonists will make almost exactly this argument, in almost exactly Locke's own vocabulary, against a king an ocean away.
