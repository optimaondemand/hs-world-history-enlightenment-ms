💡 GLOSSARY OF SUBJECT VOCABULARY
Carried over from Lesson 1.11, plus a few new terms.
deduction (dih‑DUK‑shun): reasoning from general principles down to specific conclusions, the reverse direction of induction.
rationalism (RASH‑uh‑nuh‑liz‑um): the position that reason, not sense experience, is the most reliable path to certain knowledge.
skepticism (SKEP‑tih‑siz‑um): here, the deliberate practice of doubting a belief until it proves itself certain.
induction (in‑DUK‑shun): reasoning from many specific observations toward a general conclusion, Bacon's method from last lesson.
📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
If you could doubt everything, what would be left standing?
Bacon wanted certainty built up from careful observation of the world. René Descartes, writing a generation later, tried something almost the opposite: doubting everything he possibly could, on purpose, to see what survived. What he found left standing became the starting point for an entirely different vision of how to reason, one built on clear, certain first principles rather than accumulated facts.

📚 PART 1 · DOUBTING ON PURPOSE

René Descartes was educated by Jesuits in Touraine, France, then briefly served as a soldier in the Dutch and Bavarian armies during the early years of the Thirty Years' War before settling into a quieter life of study in the Dutch Republic around 1628. There, far from the watchful eye of French censors and the university establishment, he worked out a method for reaching certainty that had almost nothing in common with Bacon's.

Descartes had reason to move carefully. He held back another book, The World, from publication after learning that the Roman Inquisition had condemned Galileo in 1633 for defending a moving Earth, a view Descartes' own book also assumed. The Discourse on Method, published four years later, was Descartes' more cautious return to print: a philosophical introduction to his method rather than a direct restatement of the astronomy that had gotten Galileo into trouble.

In his Discourse on Method, published in Leiden in 1637, Descartes describes deliberately doubting every belief he could possibly doubt, to find whatever remained too certain to question. He doubted the reports of his senses, since they sometimes deceive us. He doubted mathematical reasoning, since even careful people sometimes make errors in geometry. He even doubted whether he was awake rather than dreaming, since dreams can feel just as real as waking life while they last.

This kind of deliberate, methodical doubt, sometimes called Cartesian skepticism after Descartes' Latinized name, was not the same as simply refusing to believe anything. It was a tool: strip away every belief that could possibly be false, and see what is left.

📚 PART 2 · I THINK, THEREFORE I AM

Descartes found his unshakeable foundation in the very act of doubting itself. He reasoned that even if every one of his beliefs were false, even if the physical world, his own body, and mathematics itself were all illusions, there still had to be something doing the doubting. He could not doubt that he was thinking without that doubt itself being an act of thought. From this he concluded: cogito, ergo sum, “I think, therefore I am.” It was the one belief, he argued, that no amount of doubt could ever undermine.

From that single certain point, Descartes tried to deduce further truths step by step, the way a geometer builds an entire proof from a small set of accepted starting points. He laid out four rules for himself: accept nothing as true unless it is clearly and distinctly known to be so; break every difficult problem into smaller parts; reason from the simplest ideas up to the more complex ones, in careful order; and review the whole chain of reasoning completely enough that nothing is left out.

Because Descartes located his one certain truth in thought rather than in the body or the physical world, he concluded that the mind and the body must be two fundamentally different kinds of thing, one that thinks and one that merely occupies space. Philosophers still argue about this claim, known as Cartesian dualism, and about whether Descartes ever adequately explained how something as immaterial as a thinking mind could direct something as physical as a human body.

Descartes' method left its mark well beyond philosophy. In an appendix to the Discourse called La Géométrie, he showed how algebra and geometry, until then treated as separate fields, could be joined using a coordinate grid: the same x-axis and y-axis still taught in algebra classes today. Every time a graph plots a point using an x-coordinate and a y-coordinate, it is using a tool that traces back to Descartes' search for a single, unified method of reasoning.

Descartes spent his final months in Stockholm, having accepted an invitation from Queen Christina of Sweden to tutor her in philosophy. By most accounts she insisted on early morning lessons in a cold northern winter his health was unaccustomed to. He caught pneumonia and died in February 1650, only months after arriving, a reminder that even the most disciplined method cannot always protect the person practicing it.

Notice how differently Descartes and Bacon each answer this week's question. Bacon reasons up from facts, gathered patiently from the world outside the mind. Descartes reasons down from a single certain truth found inside the mind. Both men were responding to the same crisis of authority that Copernicus, Galileo, and Newton's generation opened up. They simply built two different roads out of it, and the political philosophers you meet beginning next week, starting with Hobbes and Locke, would borrow reasoning from both roads when they turned this same method loose on government itself.

⚠️ COMMON MISTAKE
Do not read Descartes' doubt as him actually believing the physical world might not exist. His method was a temporary, deliberate exercise to locate certainty, not his final position. By the end of his reasoning, Descartes goes on to argue that the physical world does exist after all. The doubt was a tool, not the conclusion.

💪 TWO PATHS TO SELF-GOVERNMENT

Bacon and Descartes disagreed about almost everything except one thing: neither man trusted his own comfortable assumptions to be true just because they were comfortable. Both built a discipline for testing belief against something sturdier than habit, one by going out to nature, the other by going inward to doubt. Governing your own mind well does not require choosing one path forever. It requires knowing which tool a given question actually calls for, a lesson that will matter again very soon, when Hobbes and Locke turn this same demand for certainty toward a much more dangerous question: what, if anything, makes a government legitimate in the first place.
