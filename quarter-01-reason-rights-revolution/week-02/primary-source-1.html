1.13
Bacon, Novum Organum (1620)
Primary Source
📖 BEFORE YOU READ

Bacon wrote the Novum Organum as a set of numbered aphorisms, short, dense statements meant to be read slowly and thought about individually rather than skimmed like a paragraph. The excerpts below move from Bacon's basic claim about knowledge and power to his naming of the four idols discussed in Lesson 1.11.

As you read, notice that Bacon numbers his statements with Roman numerals rather than writing connected paragraphs. Ask yourself which idol Bacon seems most worried about, and why.

💡 ABOUT THIS TRANSLATION
Bacon wrote in Latin. The text below is the standard 19th-century English translation by James Spedding, Robert Leslie Ellis, and Douglas Denon Heath, still widely used by historians of science today.
📚 FROM BOOK I OF THE NOVUM ORGANUM

“Man, being the servant and interpreter of Nature, can do and understand so much and so much only as he has observed in fact or in thought of the course of nature: beyond this he neither knows anything nor can do anything.”

Aphorism I

“Human knowledge and human power meet in one; for where the cause is not known the effect cannot be produced. Nature to be commanded must be obeyed; and that which in contemplation is as the cause is in operation as the rule.”

Aphorism III

“There are four classes of Idols which beset men's minds. To these for distinction's sake I have assigned names, calling the first class Idols of the Tribe; the second, Idols of the Cave; the third, Idols of the Market-place; the fourth, Idols of the Theatre.”

Aphorism XXXIX

“The Idols of the Tribe have their foundation in human nature itself… For it is a false assertion that the sense of man is the measure of things. On the contrary, all perceptions as well of the sense as of the mind are according to the measure of the individual and not according to the measure of the universe. And the human understanding is like a false mirror, which, receiving rays irregularly, distorts and discolours the nature of things by mingling its own nature with it.”

Aphorism XLI

“There are also Idols formed by the intercourse and association of men with each other, which I call Idols of the Market-place, on account of the commerce and consort of men there. For it is by discourse that men associate; and words are imposed according to the apprehension of the vulgar. And therefore the ill and unfit choice of words wonderfully obstructs the understanding.”

Aphorism XLIII
💡 WORDS YOU MIGHT NOT KNOW
aphorism: a short statement expressing a general truth, meant to be considered on its own.
beset: to trouble or surround persistently.
consort: here, companionship or association, not a spouse.
vulgar: in this older sense, simply “the common people,” not an insult.
