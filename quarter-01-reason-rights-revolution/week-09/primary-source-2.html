5.8
Johann Gottlieb Fichte, Addresses to the German Nation (1808)
Primary Source
📖 BEFORE YOU READ

These excerpts come from the First Address, in George Henry Turnbull's English translation, in the public domain. Fichte delivered this address in Berlin in December 1807, under French occupation.

As you read, notice how directly Fichte blames Germans themselves, not only Napoleon, for the disaster that has befallen them.

📚 FROM THE FIRST ADDRESS

“Self-seeking has destroyed itself, because by its own complete development it has lost its self and the independence of that self; and since it would not voluntarily set itself any other aim but self, an external power has forced upon it another and a foreign purpose.”

Fichte, Addresses to the German Nation, First Address, tr. Turnbull

“I speak for Germans simply, of Germans simply, not recognizing, but setting aside completely and rejecting, all the dissociating distinctions which for centuries unhappy events have caused in this single nation… it is only by means of the common characteristic of being German that we can avert the downfall of our nation.”

Fichte, Addresses to the German Nation, First Address, tr. Turnbull

“In a word, it is a total change of the existing system of education that I propose as the sole means of preserving the existence of the German nation… the new [education] must apply to every German without exception, so that it is not the education of a single class, but the education of the nation.”

Fichte, Addresses to the German Nation, First Address, tr. Turnbull

“It is, therefore, my duty… to bring courage and hope to the suffering, to proclaim joy in the midst of deep sorrow, to lead us gently and softly through the hour of deep affliction… The dawn of the new world is already past its breaking; already it gilds the mountain tops, and shadows forth the coming day.”

Fichte, Addresses to the German Nation, First Address, tr. Turnbull
💡 WORDS YOU MIGHT NOT KNOW
self-seeking: pursuing narrow private interest without regard for the common good.
dissociating distinctions: divisions that separate people who might otherwise share a common identity, here referring to regional and religious splits among Germans.
affliction: suffering or distress.
gilds: covers with a golden light, used here figuratively for the dawn.
