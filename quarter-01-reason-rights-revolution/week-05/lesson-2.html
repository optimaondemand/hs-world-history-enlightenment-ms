Lesson · Making the Case to Ordinary Readers
💡 GLOSSARY OF SUBJECT VOCABULARY
A few words worth reviewing before you read.
pamphlet: a short, unbound printed booklet, cheap and quick to produce, widely used in the 1700s to spread political argument to a broad public.
republicanism: the political belief that a state should be governed by elected representatives of the people rather than by a hereditary monarch.
hereditary succession: the practice of passing political authority down automatically through a ruling family, rather than by choice or merit.
reconciliation (in this context): the position, common in early 1776, that the colonies should seek to repair their relationship with Britain rather than separate from it.
📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
Why did an argument for independence need to reach further than Congress?
Jefferson wrote the Declaration for delegates who had, by mid-1776, already largely made up their minds. Six months earlier, in January 1776, a far less certain public still argued daily over whether reconciliation with Britain was possible or even desirable. It took a different kind of writer, and a different kind of writing, to move that argument out of Congress and into taverns, print shops, and farmhouses across the colonies.

A Failed Corset-Maker Finds His Voice

Thomas Paine was born in 1737 in Thetford, England, and had, by his own account, failed at several trades, including his father's craft of corset-making, before a chance meeting with Benjamin Franklin in London led to a letter of introduction and a fresh start in the American colonies. Paine arrived in Philadelphia in late 1774, just as the crisis between Britain and its colonies was reaching a breaking point, and quickly found work as a magazine editor, discovering a genuine talent for clear, forceful political writing.

In January 1776, Paine published Common Sense anonymously, as an inexpensive pamphlet rather than an expensive bound book, deliberately putting it within reach of ordinary readers rather than only the educated elite who typically debated political philosophy. The pamphlet sold an extraordinary number of copies within months, an estimated hundreds of thousands across a colonial population of only about two and a half million, making it, by any reasonable measure, one of the best-selling publications in American history relative to its audience.

Paine's achievement was not inventing new arguments Jefferson would later use. It was translation, in the fullest sense: taking the same Enlightenment case for natural rights and consent that educated readers already debated in Latin-inflected treatises, and rendering it in blunt, plain English aimed directly at a shopkeeper, a farmer, or a soldier trying to decide, for the first time, whether independence was something to actually want.

📚 PART 2 · AGAINST KINGS, AND AGAINST WAITING

Much of Common Sense attacks an idea most of Paine's readers had never seriously questioned: hereditary monarchy itself. Paine argued that no child is born already qualified to govern simply by being born to the right parents, comparing hereditary succession to crowning the first man met on the street, and pointed out that England's own royal line traced back to a successful invader, William the Conqueror, rather than to any special divine sanction. If monarchy's authority rested only on an accident of birth centuries removed from any actual consent, Paine asked, what genuine claim did it have on Americans now?

Paine reserved his sharpest argument, however, for the common claim that Britain was America's natural “parent country,” owed loyalty the way a child owes a parent. Even brutes, Paine wrote, do not devour their young, turning the parental metaphor directly back against Britain's treatment of the colonies. He went further still, arguing that Europe, not England specifically, was the real parent of America, since the colonies had drawn settlers fleeing persecution from across the European continent, not from England alone. If a debt was owed at all, Paine suggested, it was not owed to the country doing the persecuting.

Paine's case for urgency matched his case for principle. He argued directly against delay, insisting that reconciliation was no longer realistically available after the bloodshed already suffered at Lexington, Concord, and Bunker Hill, and that waiting only invited Britain to entangle America further in European wars it had no real stake in. “The blood of the slain,” Paine wrote, “cries, 'tis time to part.”

💡 A CLOSER LOOK AT THE HISTORY
Paine's later career is a useful check against reading him only as an American patriot. He returned to Europe in 1787, wrote Rights of Man defending the French Revolution, was made an honorary French citizen, and was later imprisoned in Paris during the Terror after falling out of favor with Robespierre's government. The same writer who helped convince Americans to break from Britain nearly lost his life to the very revolution he had championed in France, a reminder that Paine's commitment was to a set of principles, not to any one nation's cause.

📚 PART 3 · A PAMPHLET THAT CHANGED MINDS IN MONTHS

Contemporaries agreed that Common Sense moved public opinion with unusual speed. John Adams, who did not always admire Paine personally and later grew openly critical of him, still credited the pamphlet with converting a hesitant public toward independence faster than Congress itself was moving. George Washington reported that it was working a powerful change in the minds of many men in his own army. Within roughly six months of its publication, the Second Continental Congress had adopted the very Declaration of Independence you read in Lesson 3.3, a timeline historians do not treat as coincidental.

Paine's later career gives this lesson's opening question, about reaching an audience beyond the educated elite, a second act worth noticing. When he defended the French Revolution in his 1791 book Rights of Man, written against Edmund Burke's famous attack on the Revolution, Paine again wrote for as wide a readership as he could reach, convinced that the same plain argument that had moved American opinion could do the same work in Britain and France. The style was not incidental to the argument. For Paine, an idea that could not be explained plainly enough for an ordinary reader to judge for themselves was not yet doing its proper democratic work.

💪 GOVERNING A DEBATE BEFORE GOVERNING A NATION

Self-government is not only a structure of offices and laws. Before thirteen colonies could govern themselves as a nation, ordinary readers first had to govern their own minds, weighing an unfamiliar, frightening argument on its merits rather than deferring automatically to inherited loyalty. Paine's plain style was not merely a marketing choice. It was an act of trust in the ordinary reader's own capacity for self-government, the same capacity this course keeps asking you to practice.
