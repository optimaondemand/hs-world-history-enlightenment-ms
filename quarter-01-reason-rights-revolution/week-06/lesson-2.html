💡 GLOSSARY OF SUBJECT VOCABULARY
A few words worth reviewing before you read.
pamphlet: a short, cheaply printed booklet used to circulate political argument quickly and widely.
abbé (ah‑BAY): a French title for a clergyman, here referring to Sieyès's own position in the Church.
National Assembly: the body the Third Estate's delegates declared themselves to be in June 1789, claiming to represent the nation as a whole rather than one Estate among three.
privileged orders: the clergy and nobility, whose legal exemptions and inherited status set them apart from the Third Estate.
📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
What made the Third Estate's grievances a revolutionary demand rather than a request for reform?
Arthur Young showed you what the Old Regime's burden looked like on the ground. This lesson turns to the pamphlet, published just weeks before the Estates-General convened, that gave the Third Estate's frustration a single, sharp political argument: not a request for a fairer share of representation, but a claim that the Third Estate was already, in substance, the entire French nation.

📚 PART 1 · A CLERGYMAN ARGUES FOR THE COMMONERS

An Unlikely Champion of the Commoners

Emmanuel-Joseph Sieyès was, by his own official status, a member of the privileged First Estate: an abbé, a Catholic clergyman, born in 1748 in the southern French town of Fréjus. He resented, however, the way the Church's own internal hierarchy favored well-connected aristocratic clergy over commoners like himself who had entered the priesthood through more ordinary channels, and that resentment sharpened into a broader argument about privilege throughout French society.

In January 1789, just months before the Estates-General convened, Sieyès published a pamphlet titled Qu'est-ce que le Tiers État?, “What Is the Third Estate?” The pamphlet opens with a famous three-question structure that instantly clarified, for readers across France, exactly what was at stake in the coming assembly: what is the Third Estate? Everything. What has it been, until now, in the political order? Nothing. What does it ask? To become something.

That opening catechism was not merely a clever rhetorical flourish. It compressed Sieyès's entire argument into a single, memorable shape: a claim about economic and social reality (everything), a claim about political exclusion (nothing), and a demand that the two be brought into line (something). The rest of the pamphlet spends its energy proving each of those three claims in turn.

📚 PART 2 · FROM PAMPHLET TO NATIONAL ASSEMBLY

Sieyès's case for “everything” rested on a simple survey of what actually keeps a nation running. He divided useful labor into a handful of broad categories, from agriculture through manufacturing, trade, and the professions, and pointed out that the Third Estate supplied nearly all of it, while also filling the overwhelming majority of positions in the army, the courts, and the administration in practice, even when the most senior posts remained legally reserved for nobles. If the privileged orders vanished overnight, Sieyès argued, the nation would carry on almost undisturbed; if the Third Estate vanished, the nation would collapse.

Sieyès's case for “nothing” was more directly political. However large its economic contribution, the Third Estate had no real voice in a system that let two small privileged orders outvote it automatically at the Estates-General, regardless of how many delegates the commoners actually sent. A body that supplied everything and decided nothing, in Sieyès's telling, was not really represented at all; it was merely permitted to attend.

The argument did not stay confined to a pamphlet. Elected as a Third Estate delegate himself, Sieyès helped lead the push, once the Estates-General convened in May 1789, for the Third Estate's representatives to declare themselves the National Assembly in June, claiming to speak for the nation as a whole rather than for one Estate among three equals. That declaration, built directly on the logic of his own pamphlet, is the moment most historians mark as the true beginning of the French Revolution, months before the storming of the Bastille that most people picture first.

💡 A CLOSER LOOK AT THE HISTORY
Sieyès himself survived the entire Revolution and the Terror that followed it, an unusual feat for a figure this closely tied to 1789, later helping engineer the 1799 coup that brought Napoleon Bonaparte to power, whom you will meet directly in a few weeks. When later asked what he had done during the Terror's most dangerous years, Sieyès is famously said to have answered simply, “I survived,” a reminder that living through a revolution one helped start was its own kind of accomplishment.

📚 PART 3 · ONE PAMPHLET AMONG THOUSANDS

Sieyès did not write into a silent room. The relaxation of censorship around the calling of the Estates-General touched off what historians sometimes call a pamphlet war, thousands of competing pamphlets published in Paris alone in the months before May 1789, arguing over voting procedure, tax reform, and the proper limits of royal power. Most of these have been forgotten. What Is the Third Estate? was not, both because of the clarity of its central argument and because Sieyès himself went on to help enact it as an elected delegate rather than leaving it as a paper argument.

Watch, in a few weeks, for how directly this month's events feed into the next. The National Assembly that Sieyès helped bring into being in June 1789 is the same body that drafted the Declaration of the Rights of Man and of the Citizen that August, the document you will compare directly to Jefferson's Declaration of Independence. The logic of “everything, nothing, something” does not disappear once the Estates-General becomes the National Assembly. It becomes the working assumption behind everything that assembly writes next.

💪 A NATION LEARNS TO GOVERN ITSELF, IN PUBLIC

Notice that the Third Estate's decisive act was not violence. It was a declaration: a claim, argued out in a pamphlet before it was ever spoken aloud at Versailles, that the nation's real government had to match its real substance. Self-government, at this exact moment, meant a body of people refusing to keep accepting a share of power that no longer matched the share of the nation's actual work and weight they carried.
