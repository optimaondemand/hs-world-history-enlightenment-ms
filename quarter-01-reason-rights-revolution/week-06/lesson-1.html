The Old Regime and Its Discontents
Lesson · France Before 1789, Part I of II
💡 GLOSSARY OF SUBJECT VOCABULARY
A few words worth reviewing before you read.
Old Regime (also Ancien Régime): the social and political system in France before the Revolution, organized around inherited privilege rather than legal equality.
Estate: one of the three legally defined orders of French society: the clergy (First Estate), the nobility (Second Estate), and everyone else (Third Estate).
taille (TYE): a direct royal tax falling almost entirely on commoners, since the clergy and nobility were largely exempt.
seigneur (sen‑YUR): a local lord holding traditional feudal rights and dues over peasants living on his land.
📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
What made the Third Estate's grievances a revolutionary demand rather than a request for reform?
You have watched Lockean theory become a working argument for revolution in America. This week crosses the Atlantic to a country with a far older and more rigid social order. France's crisis in 1789 did not begin with a philosophical treatise. It began with a tax system that was, by nearly everyone's account, both crushing and unfair, and a government too indebted to fix it without asking its own privileged classes to give something up.

📚 PART 1 · THREE ESTATES, ONE UNEVEN BURDEN

A Society of Legal Orders, Not Legal Equals

Eighteenth-century France organized its entire population into three legally distinct Estates. The First Estate, the clergy, and the Second Estate, the hereditary nobility, together made up roughly two percent of the population but held most of France's land and enjoyed sweeping exemptions from direct taxation. The Third Estate, everyone else, from wealthy merchants and lawyers down to landless rural laborers, made up the remaining ninety-eight percent and carried nearly the entire weight of royal taxation, including the hated taille, alongside older feudal dues still owed to local seigneurs and a tithe owed to the Church.

This was not simply an accident of custom. It was the deliberate structure of a society still organized, in principle, around medieval ideas of function: the clergy prayed, the nobility fought, and the common people labored to support both. By the late 1700s, that justification had worn thin. French nobles no longer served as a standing feudal army, and a growing class of educated, prosperous commoners, lawyers, merchants, writers, increasingly found the logic of inherited privilege difficult to square with their own visible contribution to the nation's wealth.

The government's own finances turned this simmering unfairness into an urgent crisis. France's participation in the American Revolution, ironically in support of the very independence movement you studied last week, had drained the royal treasury, adding enormous debt on top of decades of prior overspending. By the 1780s, roughly half of the crown's annual revenue went simply to servicing old debt. Louis XVI's ministers proposed taxing the nobility and clergy more fairly to close the gap, and each time, the privileged orders blocked the reform through the very legal and institutional power their exempt status had always protected.

📚 PART 2 · A KING CALLS AN ASSEMBLY HE CANNOT FULLY CONTROL

With reform blocked and the treasury effectively out of options, Louis XVI made a decision no French king had made in one hundred and seventy-five years: he called the Estates-General, a national assembly of representatives from all three Estates, to meet at Versailles in May 1789. The king intended the assembly to approve new taxes and address the fiscal emergency. He did not intend, and could not have fully anticipated, how quickly the underlying question of representation itself would eclipse the tax question that had prompted the meeting in the first place.

As part of the preparation for the Estates-General, the crown invited communities across France to draw up cahiers de doléances, literally “notebooks of grievances,” recording local complaints and requests for royal attention. Tens of thousands of these documents survive, and while most stopped short of demanding revolution, together they built an enormous, detailed record of exactly how unevenly France's burdens fell, precisely the kind of evidence a traveling English farmer named Arthur Young was independently gathering with his own eyes at almost the same moment, which you will read in this week's primary source.

The immediate spark for revolution, however, came from a procedural fight rather than the grievances themselves. Voting at the Estates-General had traditionally proceeded by order, one vote per Estate, guaranteeing the privileged First and Second Estates a built-in majority over the far larger Third Estate no matter how many delegates each side actually sent. Whether that centuries-old rule would hold in 1789, or whether the Third Estate's sheer numerical and economic weight demanded something new, became the argument that turned an assembly called to fix a budget into the opening act of a revolution.

💡 A CLOSER LOOK AT THE HISTORY
It is tempting to read the French Revolution backward, as if 1789 had to end the way it did. Nothing required the Estates-General to become a revolutionary body. Reform-minded nobles and even some bishops sympathized with the Third Estate's complaints, and Louis XVI himself was not, by most accounts, the tyrant later revolutionary propaganda made him out to be. The revolution that followed grew out of specific decisions, arguments, and mistakes made by particular people in 1789, not out of any historical necessity written into France's social structure in advance.
📚 PART 3 · WHAT THE GRIEVANCE NOTEBOOKS ACTUALLY ASKED FOR

It is worth being precise about what the cahiers de doléances actually demanded, because it was, in early 1789, strikingly modest. The overwhelming majority of cahiers, including most drafted by Third Estate communities, called for a written constitution limiting royal power, regular meetings of the Estates-General, equal taxation across all three Estates, and an end to the most burdensome feudal dues, not the abolition of the monarchy, and not, in most cases, the abolition of the nobility itself. Historians studying these documents have found remarkably little demand for a republic in the modern sense anywhere in this record.

That gap between what was actually requested in early 1789 and what France became by 1793, a republic that had executed its king, is one of this course's central lessons in historical contingency. Nothing in the grievances Arthur Young recorded, or in the cahiers drawn up across the country, made a revolution against the monarchy itself inevitable. What followed grew out of a specific, escalating sequence of decisions, refusals, and fears over the next several years, not out of a demand already fully formed in 1789.

💪 SELF-GOVERNMENT DENIED, THEN DEMANDED

A tax system that lets some citizens vote themselves out of the burden others must carry is a failure of self-government in exactly the sense this course has been tracking all quarter. The Third Estate's grievance was never simply that taxes were high. It was that an entire class of French society had no real voice in deciding a burden it alone was made to bear, and no way to govern that arrangement except to ask, then eventually to demand, a seat at the table.
