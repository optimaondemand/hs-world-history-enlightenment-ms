4.7
Two Declarations Compared
Lesson · America and France, Side by Side
💡 GLOSSARY OF SUBJECT VOCABULARY
A few words worth reviewing before you read.
declaratory document: a text that formally states or announces something, such as a set of rights or an act of separation.
abstract (as used here): stated in general principle, applicable broadly, rather than tied to one specific dispute or grievance.
constituent: having the authority to establish or form a nation's basic constitutional order, as in “National Constituent Assembly.”
📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
How does the Declaration of the Rights of Man echo, and depart from, the American Declaration?
You have now read both documents closely, three weeks apart. This lesson puts them side by side on purpose, not to decide which is better, but to see clearly what each one was actually built to do, and why two documents drawing on so much of the same Enlightenment thinking still read so differently.

📚 PART 1 · TWO DIFFERENT JOBS FOR TWO DECLARATIONS
⚡ QUICK CHECK-IN
One document announces a single, specific act: leaving an empire. The other lays out permanent, general principles for a nation staying together. Which job seems harder to write well? (No wrong answer.)

One Announces an Act, the Other Lays a Foundation

Jefferson's Declaration is, at its core, a legal and moral justification for a single specific act: thirteen colonies separating from Great Britain. Its long list of grievances against George III exists to prove that this particular king, in this particular case, met Locke's standard for justified revolution. Once independence was declared, the document's immediate job was essentially finished, though its principles obviously kept working long afterward.

The Declaration of the Rights of Man was never meant to announce a single act at all. France was not separating from anyone; it was reorganizing itself internally, and the Assembly's own title for itself, National Constituent Assembly, signals the difference directly: a body with authority to lay a permanent constitutional foundation, not simply to justify one dramatic break. That is why the French document reads more like a founding law code, with numbered articles addressing arrest procedure, taxation, and freedom of speech, than like Jefferson's more narrative, case-building structure.

Even the two documents' most similar-sounding lines reward a close look. Jefferson writes that truths are “self-evident,” a phrase borrowed from the language of geometry and logic, treating equality as a premise the reader is meant to simply recognize as true once stated. The French Declaration instead simply asserts that men “are born and remain free and equal in rights,” without ever using a word like self-evident at all. The difference is subtle, but it reflects two different rhetorical strategies for the same underlying claim: one invites the reader to test the claim against their own reason, the other states it as an already-settled premise of the new legal order.

Notice, too, the difference in what each document has to prove. Jefferson has to convince readers that a specific king committed a specific pattern of abuses; most of his document is therefore evidence. The French Assembly has almost no equivalent burden, since it was not accusing any single ruler of anything specific. Its task instead was closer to Sieyès's own: stating, in the abstract, what any legitimate government must always respect, for any citizen, in any future case.

💡 A CLOSER LOOK AT THE HISTORY
It is tempting to treat the French Declaration as simply a French copy of the American one, given Lafayette's involvement and his documented consultation with Jefferson. Resist that flattening. The French Assembly had access to Montesquieu, Rousseau, and Voltaire directly, in their own language, and drew on all three explicitly in ways the American Declaration never needed to, since America's own founding argument ran almost entirely through Locke. Influence ran in both directions and through multiple channels at once, not along one single line from Philadelphia to Versailles.
📚 PART 3 · TWO DOCUMENTS, A LONG SHARED AFTERLIFE

Neither declaration stayed confined to its own century. Jefferson's language of unalienable rights and government by consent has been cited directly in independence movements from Latin America in the 1810s and 1820s to Vietnam's own 1945 declaration of independence, which quotes the American Declaration's opening lines almost verbatim before turning to its own grievances against French colonial rule. The French Declaration's numbered-article structure and its emphasis on liberty, equality, and legal protection against arbitrary arrest fed directly into later French constitutions and, many scholars argue, into the general shape of the United Nations' 1948 Universal Declaration of Human Rights.

That shared afterlife is worth sitting with, because it means the comparison you have been making all lesson is not simply an academic exercise about two eighteenth-century texts. It is a comparison between two of the most influential templates any nation has ever used to describe what it owes its own citizens, templates other nations have kept borrowing from, disagreeing with, and rewriting for more than two centuries since.

💪 GOVERNING DIFFERENTLY, TOWARD THE SAME END

Self-government does not require every nation to write the same document. It requires each nation to reckon honestly with its own specific circumstances, an empire to separate from, an old regime to dismantle, and build the argument that actually fits. Two peoples reaching similar conclusions about natural rights by two genuinely different paths is not a coincidence to explain away. It is evidence that the underlying claim, however it gets argued, was worth reaching by more than one road.
