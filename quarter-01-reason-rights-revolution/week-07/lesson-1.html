4.3
The Declaration of the Rights of Man
Lesson · The National Assembly Writes Its Founding Text
💡 GLOSSARY OF SUBJECT VOCABULARY
A few words worth reviewing before you read.
imprescriptible: a right that cannot be forfeited or taken away by the passage of time or by government action.
sovereignty: supreme political authority; the Declaration locates this in the nation as a whole rather than in the king.
general will: Rousseau's term, reused directly in the Declaration, for the collective judgment of the political community expressed through law.
public utility: benefit or usefulness to society as a whole, the only basis the Declaration allows for treating people differently under the law.
📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
How does the Declaration of the Rights of Man echo, and depart from, the American Declaration?
Six weeks after declaring itself the National Assembly, France's new governing body turned to a task the Third Estate had been building toward all year: writing down, in a single document, what rights belonged to every citizen simply by virtue of being human. The result drew on nearly every thinker you have studied this quarter, and on direct advice from a man who had helped write America's own founding declaration thirteen years earlier.

📚 PART 1 · A DECLARATION WRITTEN BY COMMITTEE, WITH AMERICAN ADVICE
⚡ QUICK CHECK-IN

Committee Work, Not a Single Author's Vision

Unlike Jefferson's Declaration, drafted mostly by one hand and then edited by a small committee, the Declaration of the Rights of Man and of the Citizen emerged from weeks of debate among hundreds of National Assembly delegates working from dozens of competing draft proposals. The Marquis de Lafayette, a French nobleman who had fought in the American Revolution under Washington, submitted an early draft directly influenced by his American experience, and historians know he consulted informally with Thomas Jefferson, then serving as the American minister to France, while preparing it. Sieyès, whose pamphlet you read last week, also shaped the final document's thinking, though the text that emerged on August 26, 1789, was ultimately the Assembly's collective work, not any one man's.

The Declaration's preamble states its purpose plainly: ignorance, forgetting, or contempt of the rights of man are the sole causes of public misery and the corruption of governments, and a solemn declaration will keep those rights constantly before every citizen's mind. Where Jefferson's Declaration argued for a specific act, separating from Britain, the French Declaration aimed at something more general: a permanent constitutional foundation for a nation that intended to remain a nation, now reorganized around citizenship rather than inherited estate.

Article 1 sets the tone for everything that follows: men are born and remain free and equal in rights, and social distinctions may be based only on public utility, not birth. This single sentence quietly abolished the entire legal logic of the Old Regime you studied last week, the very idea of three legally distinct Estates, in one stroke, though actually dismantling that system in practice would take years of further law and, eventually, violence.

Notice, too, that the preamble invokes rights recognized “in the presence and under the auspices of the Supreme Being,” language deliberately vague enough to satisfy both believing Catholics and Enlightenment deists among the delegates, without endorsing any single church's authority the way the Old Regime's own laws had endorsed Catholicism specifically. That careful, general phrasing is itself a small monument to the toleration argument Voltaire had been making for over twenty years by 1789.

📚 PART 2 · RIGHTS, LAW, AND THE GENERAL WILL

Article 2 names four rights the Declaration treats as natural and imprescriptible: liberty, property, security, and resistance to oppression, a list that echoes Locke's own formula while explicitly adding resistance to oppression as a right in itself, not merely a conclusion drawn from the others. Article 3 then makes an unmistakably Rousseauian move, locating sovereignty in the nation as a whole rather than in any king, body, or individual claiming authority the nation itself has not granted.

Article 6 goes further still, defining law itself as “the expression of the general will,” borrowing Rousseau's exact term from the Social Contract you read three weeks ago, and insisting that all citizens have a right to take part, personally or through representatives, in making it. The same article adds a Montesquieu-flavored guarantee of legal equality: the law must be the same for all, whether it protects or punishes, and all citizens are equally eligible to public office based only on their virtues and talents.

Articles 7 through 11 build out specific civil liberties in more procedural detail than Jefferson's Declaration ever attempts: protection against arbitrary arrest, the presumption of innocence, proportionate punishment defined in advance by law, and, in Article 10, a direct statement that no one should be disturbed for their opinions, including religious ones, provided their expression does not disturb the public order established by law, precisely the principle Voltaire spent an entire treatise arguing for, back in Week 4.

💡 A CLOSER LOOK AT THE HISTORY
The Declaration's promises and France's actual practice diverged almost immediately in ways worth naming honestly. Olympe de Gouges, a playwright and activist, published a Declaration of the Rights of Woman and the Female Citizen in 1791, pointedly rewriting the original article by article to argue that “man” had been read too literally and too narrowly. She was executed during the Terror in 1793. The gap between a right declared on paper and a right actually secured in practice is not a footnote to this document's history; it is a central part of it.
HISTORICAL FIGURE
Marquis de Lafayette
lah‑fee‑ETT · 1757–1834
Lafayette was a French nobleman who volunteered to fight for American independence as a young officer under Washington, then returned to France to help lead the National Assembly and submit an early draft of the Rights of Man influenced by his American experience. He later commanded the National Guard in Paris during the Revolution's early years, a position that placed him, uneasily, between the crown and the crowd as the Revolution radicalized around him.

💪 A NATION GOVERNS ITSELF BY THE WORDS IT KEEPS

Writing a right down is not the same as securing it, but it is not nothing either. A declaration creates a standard a government can be measured against, by its own citizens, including citizens the Assembly itself had not fully considered. Self-government includes the discipline of being held, later, to your own written word, even when living up to it turns out to be harder than writing it down.
