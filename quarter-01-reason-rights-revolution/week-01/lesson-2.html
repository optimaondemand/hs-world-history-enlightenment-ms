hypothesis (hy‑PAH‑thuh‑sis): an explanation proposed before it has been fully tested or proven.
Scholium (SKOH‑lee‑um): a note or comment added onto the end of a scientific or mathematical text.
empirical (em‑PEER‑ih‑kul): based on direct observation or experiment rather than on theory or authority alone.
📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
What made the apple and the moon obey the same law?
Copernicus moved the Earth. Galileo gathered evidence for the move. But neither of them explained why the planets orbit the way they do, or why anything falls at all. For nearly two thousand years, philosophy had assumed that the heavens and the Earth ran on two completely different sets of rules: perfect, unchanging circles above the moon, messy and corruptible motion below it. Isaac Newton spent much of his career erasing that line entirely. This lesson asks how one set of mathematical laws came to explain a falling apple, an orbiting moon, and the tides, all at once, and why Newton refused to say what gravity actually is.

📚 PART 1 · ONE UNIVERSE, ONE SET OF LAWS

From Woolsthorpe to the Principia

Isaac Newton was born in Woolsthorpe, England, the same year Galileo died, and studied at Cambridge. When plague closed the university in 1665, Newton returned home to Woolsthorpe for nearly two years, and it was there, working largely alone, that he developed the mathematics now called calculus and began working out how gravity might behave. Newton later told friends that watching an apple fall in his mother's orchard helped set him thinking about whether the same force pulling it downward might reach all the way to the moon. Whether the story is accurate in every detail, it captures the real leap he made: taking a force everyone already knew about on Earth and asking whether it also ran the heavens.

It took Newton another twenty years to work out the full mathematics and publish it. His book, Philosophiæ Naturalis Principia Mathematica (Mathematical Principles of Natural Philosophy), appeared in 1687. In it, Newton laid out three laws of motion and a law of universal gravitation: every object in the universe attracts every other object, with a force that depends on their masses and the distance between them. The same equation that describes an apple falling to the ground also describes the moon's orbit around the Earth and the Earth's orbit around the sun. For the first time, one mathematical law governed the heavens and the Earth together.

Newton was not the only mathematician working on these ideas. The German philosopher and mathematician Gottfried Leibniz developed calculus independently around the same time, using different notation, and a bitter priority dispute broke out between Newton's supporters and Leibniz's supporters over who deserved the credit. The argument dragged on for decades, hardened along national lines between England and the European continent, and was never fully settled to either side's satisfaction. It is a useful reminder that great discoveries are rarely made by one mind working in total isolation, whatever a tidy textbook credit line might suggest.

The gravitation law mattered for reasons beyond physics, too. Ancient and medieval philosophy had treated the region above the moon as perfect and unchanging, governed by different rules than the corruptible world below it. Newton's law did not just add a new fact. It erased the boundary between the two, and gave Copernicus's moving Earth something it had always lacked: a reason, expressed in mathematics, why the planets move the way they do.

The moon curves around the Earth instead of flying straight
Both are the same thing: an object's straight-line path being bent by gravity. The moon is always “falling” toward Earth. It simply has enough sideways speed to keep missing it.
📚 PART 2 · “I FRAME NO HYPOTHESES”

What Newton Would Not Say

Newton's law described exactly how gravity behaves: how strong it is, how it weakens with distance, how to calculate it. It did not explain what gravity actually is, or why masses attract each other at all. Some critics, particularly on the European continent, went further and accused Newton of reviving a kind of occult quality: a force acting invisibly across empty space with no physical connection between the objects involved, exactly the sort of thing mechanical philosophy at the time treated as unscientific almost by definition. Critics pressed him on this gap, and in 1713 Newton added a closing essay to a new edition of the Principia, called the General Scholium, to answer them.

His answer surprised people expecting a mechanism. Newton wrote that he had not been able to discover the cause of gravity from the evidence, and famously added, in Latin, hypotheses non fingo: “I frame no hypotheses.” He meant that he would not present an unproven guess as though it were established science, no matter how badly people wanted an explanation. It was enough, he argued, that gravity demonstrably exists and that its mathematical laws account for the motions of the planets and the tides. This was a methodological choice as much as a scientific one: description first, speculation only when the evidence actually supports it.

The same General Scholium then does something a modern reader might not expect from a founder of modern physics. Newton argued that the sheer order of the solar system, planets moving in the same direction, nearly in the same plane, at exactly the distances needed for stability, could not have arisen from mechanical causes alone. He pointed specifically to the fact that the planets all orbit in the same direction and nearly the same plane, a regularity that the law of gravity does not by itself require and cannot fully explain. He concluded that this order could “only proceed from the counsel and dominion of an intelligent and powerful being.” For Newton, discovering gravity's mathematical laws was not a step away from belief in God. He treated the order those laws revealed as evidence for exactly the opposite conclusion.

💡 A CLOSER LOOK AT THE HISTORY
Last lesson raised the idea that the Scientific Revolution is often oversimplified as a story of science replacing religion. Newton is a good reason to be careful with that story a second time. He was arguably the most important scientist of the entire period, and he treated the mathematical order he discovered as proof of a designing intelligence, not evidence against one. Whatever changed between 1500 and 1700, it was not a simple march away from religious belief.
⚡ TRY IT · PROVEN, OR BEYOND THE PROOF?
Newton was careful to separate what his mathematics actually demonstrated from what he personally believed beyond it. For each claim below, decide where it falls.
1. The force pulling an apple to the ground and the force holding the moon in orbit are the same force, obeying the same mathematical law.
2. The order of the solar system proves the existence of an intelligent, governing being.
3. Gravity acts according to precise mathematical laws, and those laws explain the ocean tides.
4. Gravity happens because an invisible fluid or spirit physically pushes objects together.

Isaac Newton
NEW‑tun · 1642–1727
Newton was an English mathematician and natural philosopher, born at Woolsthorpe and educated at Cambridge. During the plague years of the mid-1660s he developed the mathematics of calculus and began his work on motion and gravity, though he did not publish it for two more decades. His 1687 book, the Principia, laid out the three laws of motion and the law of universal gravitation, unifying the physics of Earth and the heavens for the first time. He later served as Master of the Royal Mint and president of the Royal Society, and was knighted in 1705.

💪 KNOWING THE LIMITS OF WHAT YOU KNOW

Self-government is not only about trusting your own reasoning. It is also about knowing exactly where that reasoning stops. Newton could have offered a confident-sounding guess about gravity's cause, and many readers would have accepted it simply because it came from him. He refused. Governing your own mind sometimes means answering a question with real evidence, and sometimes it means having the discipline to say, honestly, that you do not yet know. Both are forms of the same habit, and Copernicus, Galileo, and Newton each practiced a version of it in this week's lessons.
