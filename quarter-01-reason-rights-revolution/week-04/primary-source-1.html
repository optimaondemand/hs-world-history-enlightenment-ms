2.13
Montesquieu, The Spirit of Laws (1758) & Rousseau, The Social Contract (1913 tr.)
Primary Source · Two texts, paired
📖 BEFORE YOU READ

This page pairs two excerpts. The first is from Montesquieu's The Spirit of Laws, Book XI, Chapter 6, “Of the Constitution of England,” in Thomas Nugent's 1750 English translation. The second is from Rousseau's The Social Contract, Book I, Chapter 6, “The Social Compact,” in the widely used 1913 English translation by G. D. H. Cole.

As you read, watch for what each thinker treats as the real danger to liberty, and what he proposes as the cure.

📚 FROM MONTESQUIEU, THE SPIRIT OF LAWS, BOOK XI, CH. 6

“In every government there are three sorts of power: the legislative; the executive in respect to things dependent on the law of nations; and the executive, in regard to things that depend on the civil law.… The latter we shall call the judiciary power, and the other simply the executive power of the state.”

Montesquieu, The Spirit of Laws (1758 ed.), Bk. XI, Ch. 6

“When the legislative and executive powers are united in the same person, or in the same body of magistrates, there can be no liberty; because apprehensions may arise, lest the same monarch or senate should enact tyrannical laws, to execute them in a tyrannical manner. Again, there is no liberty, if the power of judging be not separated from the legislative and executive powers.”

Montesquieu, The Spirit of Laws (1758 ed.), Bk. XI, Ch. 6

“There would be an end of every thing, were the same man, or the same body, whether of the nobles or of the people, to exercise those three powers, that of enacting laws, that of executing the public resolutions, and that of judging the crimes or differences of individuals.”

Montesquieu, The Spirit of Laws (1758 ed.), Bk. XI, Ch. 6
📚 FROM ROUSSEAU, THE SOCIAL CONTRACT, BOOK I, CH. 6

“The problem is to find a form of association which will defend and protect with the whole common force the person and goods of each associate, and in which each, while uniting himself with all, may still obey himself alone, and remain as free as before. This is the fundamental problem of which the Social Contract provides the solution.”

Rousseau, The Social Contract, tr. Cole (1913), Bk. I, Ch. 6

“Each of us puts his person and all his power in common under the supreme direction of the general will, and, in our corporate capacity, we receive each member as an indivisible part of the whole.… This public person… now takes the name of Republic or body politic; it is called by its members State when passive, Sovereign when active, and Power when compared with others like itself.”

Rousseau, The Social Contract, tr. Cole (1913), Bk. I, Ch. 6

“Finally, each man, in giving himself to all, gives himself to nobody; and as there is no associate over whom he does not acquire the same right as he yields others over himself, he gains an equivalent for everything he loses, and an increase of force for the preservation of what he has.”

Rousseau, The Social Contract, tr. Cole (1913), Bk. I, Ch. 6
💡 WORDS YOU MIGHT NOT KNOW
magistrates: public officials who administer or enforce the law.
controul: an older spelling of control, as used in Nugent's 1750 translation.
associate (Rousseau's usage): a member of the political community formed by the social contract.
sovereign: here, the political community itself acting in its collective, lawmaking capacity, not a king.
