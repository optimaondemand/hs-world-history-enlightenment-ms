💡 GLOSSARY OF SUBJECT VOCABULARY
A few words worth reviewing before you read.
toleration: the practice of allowing beliefs and practices, including religious ones, that a government or majority does not itself hold or endorse.
fanaticism: an extreme, often violent devotion to a cause, here specifically a religious one, that refuses to tolerate disagreement.
Huguenot (HYOO‑guh‑not): a French Protestant, a member of the religious minority at the center of this week's primary source.
civil rights: the legal protections and freedoms owed to a person as a member of civil society, regardless of their private religious belief.
📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
What does liberty require when the disagreement is not about power, but about God?
Montesquieu divided power. Rousseau redefined obedience. Voltaire took on a narrower, sharper question: can people who genuinely believe different, mutually exclusive things about eternity live side by side in the same state without one side trying to destroy the other? In 1762, a real case in southern France turned that question from an abstract debate into a matter of life and death, and Voltaire, already Europe's most famous writer, staked his reputation on it.

The Calas Affair

In October 1761, in the city of Toulouse, a young man named Marc-Antoine Calas was found dead in his family's home. His father, Jean Calas, was a Protestant merchant, a Huguenot, living as a religious minority in a city that was almost entirely Catholic. Rumor spread quickly, without real evidence, that Marc-Antoine had planned to convert to Catholicism and that his own father had strangled him to prevent it. Local authorities, under public pressure and operating within a legal system that allowed torture to extract confessions, convicted Jean Calas. In March 1762 he was tortured and executed by being broken on the wheel, still declaring his innocence to the end.

Voltaire, already in his late sixties and Europe's most famous living writer, took up the Calas family's cause almost immediately, convinced the conviction rested on prejudice rather than proof. He organized a public campaign, funded legal appeals, and corresponded with influential figures across Europe to pressure the French crown into reopening the case. In 1765, three years after Calas's execution, the royal council formally overturned the verdict and declared him innocent, though of course too late to undo what had already been done.

It was in the middle of this campaign, in 1763, that Voltaire published A Treatise on Toleration, Occasioned by the Death of Jean Calas. The book used the Calas case as its starting point but widened quickly into a broader argument: that religious intolerance, wherever it appears and whichever faith enforces it, produces exactly this kind of injustice, and that a state has no real interest in policing what its citizens privately believe about God.

Voltaire did not fight the Calas case alone or quietly. He drew on a personal network that stretched across Europe's royal courts and salons, a web of correspondence historians sometimes call the Republic of Letters, to keep pressure on the French crown for three straight years. This was not new territory for him. He had already been imprisoned twice in the Bastille as a young man for offending powerful nobles with his writing, and he had spent 1726 to 1729 in exile in England after one such dispute turned physical. By the time of the Calas affair, Voltaire had turned that same combativeness, now backed by international fame, into a weapon aimed at the legal system itself rather than at a single rival.

📚 PART 2 · AN ARGUMENT FOR UNIVERSAL TOLERATION

Voltaire's argument in the treatise's chapter on universal toleration works less like a legal brief and more like a series of thought experiments designed to make intolerance look absurd rather than merely wrong. He imagines standing before an inquisitor and asking why the eternal salvation of a stranger's soul requires cutting out that stranger's tongue, the way, he notes dryly, no one would dream of doing to an Italian who simply spoke a different regional dialect. He imagines the vast scale of the universe, with humanity as a scattering of “imperceptible beings” on one small globe among countless others, and asks how any one of those beings can be so certain that only their own small corner of belief pleases the God of it all.

Voltaire's sharpest move is to turn the logic of persecution back on the persecutors themselves. If Christians of one denomination truly believed that a rival denomination's ordinary members were doomed to eternal punishment, he asks, how could they ever eat, trade, or even speak civilly with them in daily life? Since Christians of every stripe plainly do carry on ordinary civil relations with people of other faiths every day, Voltaire argues that their own conduct already betrays a toleration their theology claims to forbid. Persecution, in his telling, survives less on sincere belief than on habit, fear, and the convenience of those who benefit from enforcing it.

Note carefully what Voltaire is not arguing. He is not claiming that religious truth does not matter, or that all faiths are equally correct. His argument is narrower and, in a sense, more modest: that no human being is in a position to know the eternal fate of another with the certainty required to justify torturing or killing them over it in this life, and that a government claiming to act on God's behalf in that way is claiming a power that not even Voltaire's Christian opponents believed any human institution actually possessed.

💡 A CLOSER LOOK AT THE HISTORY
Voltaire had spent time in England from 1726 to 1729, in exile after a dispute with a French nobleman, and much like Montesquieu after him, came home admiring something specific about English life: not its constitution this time, but the ordinary sight of Protestants, Catholics, Jews, and Quakers doing business together on the London exchange without treating one another as enemies. Two Frenchmen crossed the same narrow stretch of water in the same few years and returned with two different lessons about the same country.

Toleration was not a side project for Voltaire; it ran through nearly everything he wrote. His short satirical novel Candide, published in 1759, mocked the same instinct to justify cruelty with tidy philosophical or religious explanations that he attacked directly in the Calas case. Voltaire later summed up his lifelong target in three French words aimed squarely at the institutional abuse of religious power, not at faith itself: Écrasez l'infâme, “crush the infamous thing.” Read alongside the treatise, the phrase clarifies what Voltaire actually wanted crushed: not belief, but the machinery that let belief become an excuse for torture.

📚 PART 3 · TOLERATION AFTER VOLTAIRE

Voltaire did not live to see the French Revolution, he died in 1778, eleven years before it began, but the argument he made for Calas did not die with him. When the French National Assembly drafted the Declaration of the Rights of Man and of the Citizen in 1789, one of its articles addressed religious opinion directly, stating that no one should be disturbed for their views, including religious ones, so long as their expression of them did not disturb the public order the law had established. You will read that Declaration in full in a few weeks. Watch, when you get there, for how much of Voltaire's argument survives inside a single sentence of law.

💪 GOVERNING BELIEF YOU DO NOT SHARE

Toleration is its own quiet form of self-government. It asks a person, or a whole state, to hold real conviction about what is true and still restrain the impulse to force that conviction on someone else by law or by violence. Voltaire did not ask Toulouse to stop being Catholic. He asked it to govern its own certainty carefully enough that certainty never again cost an innocent man his life.
