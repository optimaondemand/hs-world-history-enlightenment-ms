2.16
Voltaire, A Treatise on Toleration, Chapter XXII (1763)
Primary Source
📖 BEFORE YOU READ

Chapter XXII of Voltaire's Treatise on Toleration is titled “Of Universal Toleration.” This translation is by Joseph McCabe, published in 1912 in a volume titled Toleration and Other Essays. Voltaire wrote the full treatise in the wake of the Calas case you read about in Lesson 2.15.

As you read, notice how Voltaire imagines himself speaking directly to people he disagrees with, rather than simply arguing about them from a distance.

💡 ABOUT THIS TEXT
Voltaire wrote in French. This English translation by Joseph McCabe was published in 1912 and is in the public domain. A bracketed note in the original marks where a footnote has been omitted here for length.
📚 FROM A TREATISE ON TOLERATION, CHAPTER XXII

“One does not need great art and skilful eloquence to prove that Christians ought to tolerate each other, nay, even to regard all men as brothers. Why, you say, is the Turk, the Chinese, or the Jew my brother? Assuredly; are we not all children of the same father, creatures of the same God?”

Voltaire, A Treatise on Toleration (1763), Ch. XXII, tr. McCabe

“This little globe, which is but a point, travels in space like many other globes; we are lost in the immensity. Man, about five feet high, is certainly a small thing in the universe.… There are nine hundred million little ants like us on the earth, but my ant-hole alone is dear to God. All the others are eternally reprobated by him. Mine alone will be happy.”

Voltaire, A Treatise on Toleration (1763), Ch. XXII, tr. McCabe

“There are in Europe forty million people who are not of the Church of Rome. Shall we say to each of them: ‘Sir, seeing that you are infallibly damned, I will neither eat, nor deal, nor speak with you’?… What duty of civil life could we ever fulfil if we were really convinced that we were dealing with damned souls?”

Voltaire, A Treatise on Toleration (1763), Ch. XXII, tr. McCabe

“Are you sure that our Creator and Father will say to the wise and virtuous Confucius, to the lawgiver Solon, to Pythagoras, to Socrates, to Plato… ‘Go, monsters, go and submit to a chastisement infinite in its intensity and duration’…? You shrink with horror from such sentiments; and, now that they have escaped me, I have no more to say to you.”

Voltaire, A Treatise on Toleration (1763), Ch. XXII, tr. McCabe
💡 WORDS YOU MIGHT NOT KNOW
reprobated: condemned or rejected, here in the specific sense of condemned by God to damnation.
infallibly: without possibility of error, here used sarcastically about a claim of absolute certainty.
chastisement: punishment.
Confucius, Solon, Pythagoras, Socrates, Plato: revered philosophers and lawgivers of the ancient world who lived and died before Christianity existed, whom Voltaire uses to test whether a just God would really condemn virtuous non-Christians simply for the century they were born in.
