2.11
Montesquieu and Rousseau
Lesson · Divided Power and the General Will
💡 GLOSSARY OF SUBJECT VOCABULARY
A few words worth reviewing before you read.
separation of powers: dividing a government's legislative, executive, and judicial functions among different bodies so that no single one holds all three.
legislative power: the power to make and repeal laws.
executive power: the power to carry out laws, conduct foreign affairs, and command the state's defense.
judiciary power: the power to judge crimes and settle disputes between individuals.
general will (Rousseau's term): the collective judgment of a political community about its own common good, which a legitimate law must express.
civil liberty: freedom secured and defined by law and citizenship, as distinct from the unlimited natural liberty of the state of nature.
📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
What does liberty actually require: power kept divided, or a law each citizen has had a hand in making?
Hobbes and Locke argued about whether a people may dissolve their government. This lesson asks a different question: once a government exists, what structure keeps it from becoming a threat to the very liberty it was formed to protect? Montesquieu answers by dividing power against itself. Rousseau answers by making the law, in a sense, belong to everyone who obeys it. Both men were writing in the same century, in the same language, about the same problem, and they answered it in strikingly different ways.

📚 PART 1 · MONTESQUIEU AND THE POWER THAT CHECKS ITSELF
⚡ QUICK CHECK-IN

A French Nobleman Studies an English Habit

Charles-Louis de Secondat, Baron de Montesquieu, was born in 1689 near La Brède, France, into a family of the noble class known as the nobility of the robe, judges and magistrates rather than warriors. He inherited both a title and a seat on a regional court, giving him a working lawyer's eye for how institutions actually behave, not just how they are supposed to behave on paper.

Around 1729, Montesquieu traveled to England and spent nearly two years there, watching Parliament debate, reading English law, and talking with English statesmen. He returned to France convinced that England had stumbled onto something rare: a government where power was carved into separate pieces, each piece jealous enough of the others to keep any one of them from swallowing the rest. In 1748, after roughly twenty years of research, he published his life's major work, The Spirit of the Laws, and it is Book XI, Chapter 6, describing the English constitution, that made him famous across Europe and, later, in America.

Montesquieu's central claim was simple to state and hard to arrange in practice: political liberty is not a feeling, but a structural fact. It exists only where the legislative power (making laws), the executive power (carrying them out and conducting foreign affairs), and the judiciary power (judging crimes and disputes) are held by different hands. Combine any two of the three in the same person or body, he argued, and tyranny becomes not just possible but likely, whatever the ruler's intentions.

📚 PART 2 · A GOVERNMENT AFRAID OF ITSELF, ON PURPOSE

Montesquieu's reasoning ran through each pairing in turn. Join the legislative and executive powers, and whoever makes the laws can also enforce them however it pleases, since the same hand writes the rule and swings the hammer. Join the judicial power to the legislative, and a judge becomes a lawmaker able to bend any case to their own will. Join the judicial power to the executive, and a judge gains the coercive force of the state behind every private grudge. In each case, Montesquieu argued, the danger is not that a tyrant seizes power dramatically. It is that ordinary, fallible people accumulate enough of it, quietly, that no one is left to check them.

His solution, drawn from what he believed he had observed in England, gave each power to a different body: a king to execute the laws, an elected assembly representing the common people to make them, and a body of nobles both to share in lawmaking and to serve as a check on the elected assembly. Crucially, no branch could do everything a government needs done. Each depended on the others, and each could block the others from overreaching, producing what Montesquieu called a state of motion held in careful tension rather than a single hand pulling every lever.

💡 A CLOSER LOOK AT THE HISTORY
Historians have long noted that Montesquieu's England was, in places, more of an idealized model than a photograph. By the 1720s, English government already leaned on a cabinet of ministers who blurred his tidy line between the lawmaking Parliament and the law-executing crown. Montesquieu's real achievement was not perfect reporting. It was turning an admired, imperfect example into a portable argument, one clear enough that the framers of the American Constitution could build directly on it a generation later.
HISTORICAL FIGURE
Baron de Montesquieu
MON‑tess‑kyoo · 1689–1755
Montesquieu was a French magistrate and philosopher whose 1748 book The Spirit of the Laws argued that political liberty requires dividing legislative, executive, and judicial power among separate hands. His account of the English constitution, drawn from nearly two years spent observing it firsthand, became one of the most influential arguments in the history of constitutional government.
📚 PART 3 · ROUSSEAU AND THE WILL THAT BELONGS TO EVERYONE

Jean-Jacques Rousseau came from a different world than Montesquieu's noble estate. Born in 1712 in the small, proudly self-governing republic of Geneva, the son of a watchmaker, Rousseau spent much of his adult life wandering France, writing on music, education, and politics, and quarreling, often bitterly, with the very Enlightenment thinkers he is now grouped alongside. In 1762 he published Of the Social Contract, a slim book that asked a question distinct from Hobbes's or Locke's: not whether government could be justified, but what would make a law truly the people's own, rather than a rule merely imposed on them from outside.

Rousseau's answer centered on a single, difficult idea: the general will. When a community forms a political body, Rousseau argued, each person gives up their natural, unlimited freedom and receives something new in exchange: a share in a collective will aimed at the common good of the whole community, not the sum of everyone's private wants. A law is legitimate, on this view, only when it genuinely expresses that general will, which is why Rousseau insisted the people themselves, not a representative acting at a distance, must be the ones who will the law.

This produced Rousseau's most famous, most argued-over claim: obeying a law you had a real hand in making is not a loss of freedom but a different, higher kind of freedom, what he called civil liberty replacing mere natural liberty. Critics then and since have asked an uncomfortable follow-up question: what happens to someone who disagrees with the general will? Rousseau's own answer, that such a person may in a sense be “forced to be free,” has struck many later readers as either a profound insight about self-government or a dangerous opening for a majority, or a faction claiming to speak for it, to crush a dissenting minority in freedom's own name.

HISTORICAL FIGURE
Jean-Jacques Rousseau
roo‑SO · 1712–1778
Rousseau was a Genevan-born philosopher whose 1762 book Of the Social Contract argued that legitimate law must express a community's general will, and that obeying such a law is itself a form of freedom. His ideas would echo loudly a generation later, invoked by revolutionaries in France who read the general will as a mandate for their own version of the common good.
📚 PART 4 · TWO IDEAS THAT OUTLIVED THEIR AUTHORS

Montesquieu and Rousseau never met as rivals in quite the way Hobbes and Locke can be paired, but history did the pairing for them a generation later. When American delegates gathered in Philadelphia in 1787, Montesquieu was the single most quoted authority in their debates, cited by name in the Federalist Papers as the source for dividing the new federal government into legislative, executive, and judicial branches. Rousseau's fate was stranger and darker. When French revolutionaries began remaking their own government just two years later, Maximilien Robespierre and other leaders of the Terror invoked the general will constantly, treating it as license to silence and execute anyone judged an enemy of the people's supposed collective interest.

Neither outcome was guaranteed by the original texts alone. Montesquieu's admiring, somewhat idealized reading of England became a workable blueprint partly by accident of timing and audience. Rousseau's demanding, communal vision of freedom became a justification for terror partly because later men chose to read it that way under the pressure of a revolution he did not live to see. Keep both outcomes in mind as you move toward the French Revolution in a few weeks. The ideas you are reading this week did not cause what came later by themselves; people, acting in particular circumstances, chose what to do with them.

💪 GOVERNING POWER BY DESIGN, NOT BY HOPE

Notice what Montesquieu and Rousseau share, underneath their disagreement. Neither man asks citizens to simply trust that power will be used well. Montesquieu builds distrust directly into the architecture of government, so that no single office ever has to be trusted completely. Rousseau builds self-government into the very definition of law, so that obedience and freedom stop being opposites. Self-government, this quarter's virtue, is not a feeling of independence. It is a structure, or a habit, deliberately built so that liberty does not depend on any one person's good intentions, including your own.
