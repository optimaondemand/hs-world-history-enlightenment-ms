The Ottoman Empire and the Tanzimat
Lesson · Empires of Many Peoples, Part II of II
💡 GLOSSARY OF SUBJECT VOCABULARY
A few words worth reviewing before you read.
Tanzimat: Turkish for "reorganization," the name given to the Ottoman Empire's era of reform from 1839 to 1876, aimed at modernizing law, administration, and the status of non-Muslim subjects.
Sultan: the title of the Ottoman Empire's monarch.
Sublime Porte: the traditional name for the Ottoman imperial government, referring to a gate at the entrance of a government building in Constantinople.
millet: a semi-autonomous religious community within the Ottoman Empire, each governed partly by its own religious leaders under overall Ottoman sovereignty.
📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
Can an empire of many peoples hold together once nationalism becomes a rival loyalty?
Like Russia, the Ottoman Empire ruled an enormous variety of peoples, religions, and languages under one throne, and like Russia, its rulers turned to reform in the mid-nineteenth century to try to hold that variety together. This lesson examines the Ottoman reform program known as the Tanzimat, and its most sweeping single document, the 1856 decree promising full legal equality to every Ottoman subject regardless of religion.

📚 PART 1 · REFORM UNDER PRESSURE

From Gulhane to Paris

The Ottoman Empire began its own era of reform, known as the Tanzimat, in 1839 with a decree issued at Gulhane promising legal reforms and better treatment for all subjects. Like Russia, the Ottoman government faced mounting pressure from both internal unrest, especially nationalist uprisings among its Christian Balkan subjects, and from more powerful European states eager to use the treatment of Ottoman Christians as a pretext for intervening in Ottoman affairs, as seen in Lord Palmerston's suspicions about Russia's motives before the Crimean War.

The Ottoman Empire's 1856 victory alongside Britain and France in the Crimean War, rather than Russia's defeat, became the occasion for the empire's most sweeping reform decree. As a condition connected to the peace settlement, and to remove the pretext for further European interference on behalf of Christian subjects, Sultan Abdulmejid I issued the Hatt-i Humayun in February 1856, promising full legal equality to every Ottoman subject regardless of religion.

This week's primary source presents the decree's own sweeping language. Read closely for exactly what equality it promises, and consider what would actually be required to make such a promise real across an empire as vast and diverse as the Ottoman domains.

📚 PART 2 · PROMISE AND PRACTICE

The Hatt-i Humayun's promises were genuinely sweeping on paper: equal eligibility for public office and military service regardless of religion, equal taxation, reformed courts with mixed religious representation, and an explicit ban on language demeaning any subject because of religion, language, or race. In practice, implementation proved far harder than proclamation. Non-Muslim subjects continued to face social and administrative discrimination in daily life, converting the decree's legal promises only partially into lived reality.

More importantly for this module's central question, legal equality did not resolve the deeper problem of rising nationalism among the empire's Christian populations. Serbian, Greek, Bulgarian, and Romanian nationalists increasingly wanted not equal status within the Ottoman Empire but independence from it entirely, a demand no amount of legal reform inside Ottoman institutions could satisfy, since it rejected the legitimacy of those institutions altogether.

The Tanzimat reforms continued through 1876, when a new Ottoman constitution briefly introduced a parliament, quickly suspended by Sultan Abdulhamid II. Like Alexander II's Great Reforms, the Ottoman Tanzimat represented real, serious change that nonetheless failed to resolve the empire's underlying multinational strain, a strain that would ultimately contribute to the Balkan wars and the empire's continued territorial losses into the twentieth century.

💡 A CLOSER LOOK AT THE HISTORY
Russia, the Ottoman Empire, and Austria-Hungary, which this module has not covered in equal depth but which faced an almost identical strain among its own Slavic, Hungarian, and German-speaking populations, are often grouped together by historians as multinational empires unable to fully accommodate nineteenth-century nationalism. Each pursued its own distinct strategy, autocratic reform in Russia, legal equality in the Ottoman Empire, negotiated power-sharing in Austria-Hungary's 1867 Compromise, but none fully solved the underlying tension between imperial unity and national self-determination.

📚 PART 3 · THIS MODULE'S CLOSING QUESTION

Set Russia and the Ottoman Empire side by side, and a pattern emerges that this module's discussion will ask you to weigh directly. Both empires recognized, under real internal and external pressure, that their treatment of large portions of their own population needed to change. Both produced genuinely significant reform. Neither reform resolved the deeper strain of holding together peoples who increasingly wanted, in one form or another, a political future defined by their own nationality rather than by inclusion in someone else's empire.

This is the same nationalist force this quarter traced building nations in Germany and Italy, now viewed from the opposite side, as a force straining empires apart rather than knitting nations together. Whether that force made the multinational empire an obsolete form of government by the century's end, or merely one more problem careful statesmanship might still have managed, is a genuinely open historical question, not a settled verdict.

💪 A LAW PROMISED IS A RESPONSIBILITY TO ENFORCE IT

The Hatt-i Humayun's sweeping language of equality was a genuine act of stated responsibility, an empire committing itself in writing to treat all its subjects as equally worthy of protection. But a government's responsibility does not end at proclamation. Where implementation lagged behind the promise, as it did here, the gap itself became a further source of the very distrust the decree was meant to resolve.
