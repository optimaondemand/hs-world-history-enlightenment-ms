💡 GLOSSARY OF SUBJECT VOCABULARY
A few words worth reviewing before you read.
archduke: a title held by senior princes of the Austrian imperial house.
ultimatum: a final set of demands whose rejection is understood to lead to serious consequences, often war.
annexation: the formal incorporation of one territory into another state's control.
Narodna Odbrana (NAH‑rod‑nah ohd‑BRAH‑nah): a Serbian nationalist organization the Austro-Hungarian government accused of supporting the assassination plot.
📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
Was the Great War an accident, or was Europe primed to want it?
On June 28, 1914, a Bosnian Serb nationalist shot and killed Archduke Franz Ferdinand, heir to the Austro-Hungarian throne, during a visit to Sarajevo. This lesson traces how Austria-Hungary responded, not with a limited police action, but with a deliberately harsh ultimatum to Serbia, designed, by the admission of some of its own authors, to be difficult for Serbia to accept in full.

The Assassination

On June 28, 1914, Archduke Franz Ferdinand, heir to the Austro-Hungarian throne, visited Sarajevo, the capital of Bosnia-Herzegovina, a province Austria-Hungary had formally annexed in 1908 over strong Serbian objections. A group of young Bosnian Serb nationalists, several connected to a Serbian nationalist organization, had traveled to Sarajevo with bombs and pistols supplied from Belgrade to assassinate him. After an initial bomb attack failed, one of the conspirators, Gavrilo Princip, shot and killed both Franz Ferdinand and his wife, Sophie, when their car unexpectedly stopped near where he was standing.

Austria-Hungary's subsequent judicial investigation, presented as an official annex to its later communications with other powers, concluded that the plot had been organized in Belgrade, that the conspirators' weapons had come from a Serbian army arms depot, and that Serbian border officials had helped smuggle the assassins and their weapons across the frontier into Bosnia. This week's primary source presents that investigation's own findings, worth reading for exactly what it does and does not claim about the Serbian government's own direct responsibility.

Whatever the investigation's findings actually proved about official Serbian involvement, a question historians still debate, Austria-Hungary's government used the assassination as the occasion for a much broader confrontation with Serbia, one many Austrian officials had wanted for years, over Serbian nationalism's threat to the multinational empire covered in Module 9.

📚 PART 2 · A DEMAND DESIGNED TO BE REFUSED

On July 23, 1914, nearly a month after the assassination, Austria-Hungary delivered a formal ultimatum to Serbia, containing ten specific demands, including the suppression of anti-Austrian organizations, the removal of implicated officials, and, most controversially, the direct participation of Austro-Hungarian officials in Serbia's own internal investigation of the plot. Serbia was given only forty-eight hours to respond.

The demand for Austro-Hungarian officials to participate directly in Serbian judicial proceedings struck many European diplomats at the time, and most historians since, as a deliberate infringement on Serbian sovereignty that Austria-Hungary's own leaders expected, and in some cases hoped, Serbia would refuse. Serbia's actual reply accepted nearly all of the ultimatum's demands, with careful qualifications on the sovereignty-infringing points, an unexpectedly conciliatory response that nonetheless failed to prevent what followed.

Austria-Hungary declared Serbia's reply unsatisfactory and declared war on July 28, 1914, exactly one month after the assassination. What happened next did not stay contained to Austria-Hungary and Serbia, a fact Lesson 10.7 traces directly through the alliance system this quarter has already introduced in Modules 8 and 9.

💡 A CLOSER LOOK AT THE HISTORY
Historians continue to debate exactly how much Austria-Hungary's leadership wanted war outright versus a limited, contained punishment of Serbia. Both readings agree the ultimatum's specific terms were chosen deliberately to be difficult to accept, evidence against treating what followed as a pure, unintended accident, even while the war's eventual scale, drawing in nearly every major power, was not something any single government in July 1914 fully planned or wanted.
HISTORICAL FIGURE
Archduke Franz Ferdinand
frants FER‑dih‑nand · 1863–1914
Franz Ferdinand was heir presumptive to the Austro-Hungarian throne, nephew of Emperor Franz Joseph I. His assassination in Sarajevo on June 28, 1914, alongside his wife Sophie, became the immediate trigger for the July Crisis, though he had himself favored greater autonomy for the empire's Slavic populations, a policy some historians argue his death actually foreclosed rather than advanced.
📚 PART 3 · A MONTH OF SILENCE, THEN AN ULTIMATUM

One easily overlooked detail matters for judging Austria-Hungary's intentions: nearly a full month passed between the assassination on June 28 and the ultimatum's delivery on July 23. That delay was not spent on further investigation alone. Austrian officials spent much of it privately securing Germany's commitment to support Austria-Hungary regardless of Russia's reaction, a commitment historians call the "blank check," before finalizing the ultimatum's harshest terms.

This sequence, investigation, private great-power commitment, then ultimatum, suggests a calculated process rather than a hasty reaction to a shocking crime. Keep this timeline in mind heading into Lesson 10.7, which follows what happened once Austria-Hungary's calculation met the alliance commitments of every other great power in Europe.

💪 A DEMAND'S TERMS REVEAL ITS TRUE PURPOSE

A government responsible for the consequences of an ultimatum owes a truthful account of what that ultimatum actually intends, not just what it says it intends. Austria-Hungary's specific choice of demands, not the fact of demanding accountability itself, is what reveals whether its stated goal, justice for the assassination, was its real one.
