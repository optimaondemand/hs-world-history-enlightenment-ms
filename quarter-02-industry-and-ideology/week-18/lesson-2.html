💡 GLOSSARY OF SUBJECT VOCABULARY
A few words worth reviewing before you read.
alliance: a formal agreement between states to support one another, often militarily, under specified conditions.
mobilization: the process of organizing and readying a nation's armed forces for war, treated by military planners in 1914 as extremely difficult to reverse once begun.
Triple Alliance: the alliance linking Germany, Austria-Hungary, and Italy before 1914.
Triple Entente: the looser set of agreements linking France, Russia, and Britain before 1914.
📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
Was the Great War an accident, or was Europe primed to want it?
Austria-Hungary's war with Serbia did not need to become a general European war. This lesson traces exactly how it did, through a network of alliances meant to deter aggression that instead pulled one great power after another into the conflict, and through a remarkable private correspondence between two monarchs who were, by their own words, trying to stop the very war their governments' actions were making harder to avoid.

Two Rival Blocs

By 1914, Europe's great powers had organized themselves into two alliance systems. The Triple Alliance linked Germany, Austria-Hungary, and Italy. The Triple Entente loosely linked France, Russia, and Britain, built substantially around France and Russia's shared concern about German power, itself a legacy of Germany's 1871 unification and annexation of Alsace-Lorraine covered in Module 8. These alliances were originally intended as defensive, deterring aggression by making any single attack a war against multiple powers simultaneously.

When Austria-Hungary declared war on Serbia on July 28, 1914, that deterrent logic began working in reverse. Russia, bound by its own ties of Slavic and Orthodox solidarity with Serbia and by its alliance with France, began mobilizing its army in support of Serbia. Germany, allied to Austria-Hungary, viewed Russian mobilization as an intolerable threat and declared war on Russia on August 1, then on France, Russia's ally, two days later. Germany's invasion of neutral Belgium en route to France then brought Britain into the war as well, honoring its own treaty obligations to Belgian neutrality.

Within roughly one week, a regional dispute between Austria-Hungary and Serbia had become a war engulfing every major European power. This week's primary source lets you examine one especially revealing piece of evidence about whether this outcome was fully intended by the people closest to it.

📚 PART 2 · COUSINS WHO COULD NOT STOP IT

Kaiser Wilhelm II of Germany and Tsar Nicholas II of Russia were first cousins, personally acquainted, and had exchanged friendly private letters and telegrams for years, sometimes signing them with the nicknames "Willy" and "Nicky." As the July Crisis escalated, the two monarchs exchanged a remarkable series of direct telegrams between July 29 and August 1, 1914, each expressing genuine desire to avoid a general war, even as their own governments' military mobilization plans moved forward largely outside their personal control.

This correspondence is genuinely striking evidence for this module's central question. Neither monarch, at least in his own words, wanted the catastrophic general war that followed. Yet neither proved able, or in some readings fully willing, to halt the specific military machinery, mobilization timetables, alliance commitments, and mutual distrust, that their own governments had built over the preceding decades. Contingency and structure both mattered: different personal choices at key moments might have changed the outcome, but the alliance and mobilization system itself made avoiding war progressively harder as each day of the crisis passed.

War was declared regardless. Whether that outcome reflects the telegrams' sincerity being overwhelmed by forces beyond either ruler's control, or reflects a deeper willingness for war lurking beneath the diplomatic courtesy, is exactly the kind of question this course asks you to weigh using the actual evidence, not a predetermined verdict either way.

💡 A CLOSER LOOK AT THE HISTORY
This course has emphasized contingency over inevitability throughout the semester, and the July Crisis is one of the strongest test cases for that approach. Historians still actively debate how avoidable the general war actually was, whether different decisions at several specific points, a different German response to Russian mobilization, a firmer British commitment stated earlier, could plausibly have kept the conflict contained to Austria-Hungary and Serbia. No single, settled answer commands full historical consensus, which is itself a lesson in how history actually works.

HISTORICAL FIGURE
Kaiser Wilhelm II
VIL‑helm the SEC‑und · 1859–1941
Wilhelm II reigned as German Emperor from 1888 until his forced abdication in 1918, following Germany's defeat in the war that began in 1914. A grandson of Britain's Queen Victoria and a first cousin of both Britain's King George V and Russia's Tsar Nicholas II, Wilhelm's personal telegrams during the July Crisis reveal a ruler who claimed to seek peace even as Germany's own military planning pushed toward war.
📚 PART 3 · CLOSING A SEMESTER OF CONTINGENCY

This semester began with the Scientific Revolution's confidence that careful reasoning could master the natural world, and it ends at a precipice where careful private reasoning between two rulers could not master the political world their own governments had built. That is not a contradiction this course resolves for you. It is the deliberate final note of a semester that has tried, again and again, to resist easy stories of inevitable progress or inevitable doom.

Quarter 3 picks up the war this module stops just short of. 💪 A SEMESTER'S CLOSING QUESTION ABOUT RESPONSIBILITY

This quarter's virtue, responsibility, closes on its hardest case yet. Wilhelm and Nicholas each claimed responsibility for seeking peace, in writing, while the machinery each had built, alliances, mobilization plans, military timetables, carried events past the point either could fully control. Responsibility exercised only in private correspondence, without matching action to restrain the systems already in motion, is a caution this course leaves you to weigh for yourself as the semester closes.
