💡 GLOSSARY OF SUBJECT VOCABULARY
A few words worth reviewing before you read.
Risorgimento (ree‑sor‑jee‑MEN‑toh): Italian for "resurgence" or "rising again," the name given to the nineteenth-century movement to unify the Italian peninsula into a single nation.
nationalism: the belief that a people who share language, culture, and history form a natural nation entitled to its own self-governing state.
plebiscite: a direct vote of the whole population on a single political question, used repeatedly during Italian unification to ratify territorial changes.
annexation: the formal incorporation of one territory into another state.
📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
Is a nation made by shared ideals, or by conquest and iron?
While Bismarck was forging Germany through calculated war, a very different mix of ideals, diplomacy, and improvised military adventure was unifying Italy on almost the same timeline. This lesson introduces the three men most responsible for the Risorgimento: a romantic nationalist prophet who wrote and agitated for Italian unity for decades before it happened, a shrewd prime minister who maneuvered great powers into helping, and a soldier of fortune whose private army did what neither of the other two could do alone.

A Prophet of Italian Unity

Giuseppe Mazzini spent most of his adult life in exile, expelled from one Italian state after another for his revolutionary activity, yet he never stopped arguing, writing, and organizing for a single unified Italian nation. Mazzini's vision of nationhood went well beyond a simple demand for independence from foreign or local rulers. For Mazzini, a nation was fundamentally a moral community, a group of people bound together by shared history, language, and duty, whose members owed each other, and owed humanity as a whole, the responsibility of building a free and unified homeland.

Mazzini founded a secret society called Young Italy in 1831, dedicated to spreading the ideal of a unified, republican Italian nation among the young men of the peninsula. Though Young Italy's own uprisings mostly failed, and Mazzini himself never held political power in the unified Italy that eventually emerged, his relentless moral argument for Italian nationhood shaped an entire generation of Italian patriots, including men who would go on to actually accomplish what Mazzini could only advocate for.

This week's primary source is drawn from an 1848 address Mazzini delivered in Milan, commemorating Italian patriots executed years earlier for attempting an uprising. Notice, as you read it, how insistently Mazzini frames the struggle for Italian unity as a matter of love, duty, and moral obligation, not merely strategy or self-interest.

📚 PART 2 · CAVOUR'S DIPLOMACY AND GARIBALDI'S ARMY

Mazzini's ideals alone did not unify Italy. That task fell substantially to two very different men. Camillo di Cavour, prime minister of the northern Kingdom of Sardinia-Piedmont from 1852, was a shrewd, pragmatic statesman with little of Mazzini's revolutionary fervor. Cavour modernized Piedmont's economy and army, then maneuvered the kingdom into an alliance with France against Austria in 1859, a war that added the wealthy region of Lombardy to Piedmont's territory and set off a chain reaction of uprisings and annexations across central Italy.

The most dramatic single episode of unification, however, came from neither Mazzini's ideals nor Cavour's diplomacy directly, but from Giuseppe Garibaldi, a soldier and adventurer who had once belonged to Mazzini's Young Italy movement. In 1860, Garibaldi led roughly one thousand volunteer fighters, known as the Redshirts, in a daring invasion of Sicily and then the southern Italian mainland, defeating the much larger forces of the Kingdom of the Two Sicilies and handing his conquests over to King Victor Emmanuel II of Piedmont rather than keeping power for himself. Cavour, who had not authorized Garibaldi's expedition and worried it might provoke foreign intervention, worked quickly afterward to fold Garibaldi's conquests into the emerging Italian kingdom through plebiscites rather than let them become a separate, rival power.

The Kingdom of Italy was proclaimed in March 1861, with Victor Emmanuel II as its first king. Cavour, who died only months later, spent his final energies arguing that Rome, still under papal control and protected by French troops, had to become Italy's capital for the new nation to have any real stability, a claim central to this week's second primary source once you reach it.

💡 A CLOSER LOOK AT THE HISTORY
Italian unification is often told as a single triumphant story, but the men who built it did not fully trust each other, and their visions of what a unified Italy should be, republican and popular for Mazzini, a constitutional monarchy managed by an educated elite for Cavour, differed sharply. The Italy that actually emerged in 1861 was closer to Cavour's vision than Mazzini's, a genuinely contingent outcome rather than history's only possible ending.
HISTORICAL FIGURE
Giuseppe Mazzini
joo‑ZEP‑eh mat‑SEE‑nee · 1805–1872
Mazzini was an Italian nationalist and revolutionary who spent much of his life in exile, founding the Young Italy movement in 1831 and writing extensively on nationalism as a moral duty. Though he never held power in the unified Italy that emerged, his writing shaped the ideals of the entire Risorgimento generation, including Garibaldi.
📚 PART 3 · TWO PATHS TO THE SAME DECADE

Set beside Lesson 8.3, Italy's story looks like a genuine counterpoint to Germany's. Bismarck unified Germany chiefly through Prussian state power and calculated war, with popular nationalist sentiment following behind the fact of unification rather than driving it. Italy's unification drew far more directly on decades of popular nationalist agitation, Mazzini's foremost among it, even though the final political shape of the new kingdom still depended heavily on Cavour's diplomacy and Garibaldi's improvised military campaign.

Neither story fully supports a single clean answer to this week's question. Ideals mattered enormously in Italy, but so did Cavour's cold calculation and Garibaldi's willingness to fight. Force mattered enormously in Germany, but Bismarck still needed genuine German national sentiment to make the new empire hold together once it existed. Keep both cases in view as this week's Critical Questions asks you to weigh them against each other directly.

💪 A NATION'S FOUNDING GENERATION ANSWERS FOR ITS SHAPE

Mazzini, Cavour, and Garibaldi each bore responsibility for what unified Italy would actually become, not merely for whether it would exist. Mazzini's republican ideal did not fully survive into the constitutional monarchy Cavour built, and Garibaldi's own personal ambition, restrained by his choice to hand his conquests over rather than keep them, mattered as much as his battlefield success. Founding a nation well requires responsibility for its lasting shape, not credit for its founding moment alone.
