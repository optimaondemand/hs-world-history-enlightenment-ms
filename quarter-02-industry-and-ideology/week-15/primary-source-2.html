8.16
John A. Hobson, Imperialism: A Study (1902)
Primary Source · Chapter VI, "The Economic Taproot of Imperialism"
📖 BEFORE YOU READ

Hobson's 1902 book is in the public domain; this excerpt is drawn from a digitized scan whose original-language spelling is correct but whose OCR (optical character recognition) scan introduced letter errors, since silently corrected without altering any wording.

The first passage below is Hobson's own summary of the economic argument imperialists made in their own defense, which he presents before responding to it. The second passage is Hobson's own conclusion. Read the first passage as someone else's case, quoted, not Hobson's personal opinion.

📚 FROM IMPERIALISM: A STUDY

“We must have markets for our growing manufactures, we must have new outlets for the investment of our surplus capital and for the energies of the adventurous surplus of our population: such expansion is a necessity of life to a nation with our great and growing powers of production. However costly, however perilous, this process of imperial expansion may be, it is necessary to the continued existence and progress of our nation. Imperialism is thus seen to be, not a choice, but a necessity.”

John A. Hobson, Imperialism: A Study (1902), quoting the imperialists' own argument

“The economic root of Imperialism is the desire of strong organised industrial and financial interests to secure and develop at the public expense and by the public force private markets for their surplus goods and their surplus capital. War, militarism, and a ‘spirited foreign policy’ are the necessary means to this end.”

John A. Hobson, Imperialism: A Study (1902), Hobson's own conclusion
💡 WORDS YOU MIGHT NOT KNOW
outlets (as Hobson uses it): places or markets where surplus goods or money can be productively used or invested.
a "spirited foreign policy": a phrase Hobson quotes ironically, referring to an assertive, expansionist approach to foreign affairs.
at the public expense: paid for by ordinary taxpayers, as opposed to the private interests Hobson argues actually benefit.
