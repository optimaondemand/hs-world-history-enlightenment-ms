Empire's Idea of Itself
Lesson · The Age of Empire, Part I of II
💡 GLOSSARY OF SUBJECT VOCABULARY
A few words worth reviewing before you read.
imperialism: the policy of extending a nation's power and influence over other territories and peoples, usually through direct colonial rule.
colony: a territory under the political control of a distant governing power, typically settled or administered for that power's benefit.
paternalism: treating people in a fatherly, protective way that also assumes they are not capable of governing or deciding for themselves.
civilizing mission: the claim, common among nineteenth-century imperial powers, that colonial rule brought progress, religion, and modern institutions to colonized peoples.
📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
What did empire tell itself it was doing, and which justifications were sincere, which convenient?
By the close of the nineteenth century, European nations, joined increasingly by the United States, controlled vast overseas territories across Africa and Asia. This lesson asks what these empires told themselves, and told the watching world, about why they were doing this. The most famous single piece of that self-justification is a poem written by one of the era's most popular writers, urging the United States to take up what he called a burden.

📚 PART 1 · A POEM WRITTEN FOR A SPECIFIC MOMENT

Kipling and the Philippines

In 1899, the British writer Rudyard Kipling, already famous for novels and poems set in colonial India, published a poem titled The White Man's Burden, addressed not to his own country but to the United States. The occasion was the Philippine-American War, which broke out after the United States, having just defeated Spain in 1898, chose to retain the Philippines as a colonial possession rather than grant it independence, provoking a Filipino independence movement into armed resistance.

Kipling's poem urges the United States to accept colonial rule as a duty rather than a prize, framing the work of governing "new-caught, sullen peoples" as thankless, difficult, and morally necessary rather than profitable or glorious. This framing, that empire was a burden reluctantly shouldered for the benefit of the colonized rather than the colonizer, became one of the most widely quoted expressions of what contemporaries called the civilizing mission: the claim that colonial rule spread religion, education, modern government, and technological progress to peoples imperial powers considered less developed.

The poem was reprinted immediately and widely across American newspapers, entering public debate over the Philippines within days of its publication. It was quoted approvingly by supporters of American expansion and mocked or reversed by critics, some of whom wrote parody versions attacking the very policy it defended. Few poems in the English language have been cited so often, by so many sides of a single political argument, as evidence for their own position.

📚 PART 2 · BEYOND THE CIVILIZING MISSION

The civilizing mission was not the only justification imperial powers offered, and honest historical analysis requires naming the others. Strategic justifications pointed to naval coaling stations, secure trade routes, and denying territory to rival powers. Religious justifications, especially prominent among missionary societies, framed empire as an opportunity to spread Christianity. National prestige mattered enormously too; European statesmen openly treated the size of a nation's colonial empire as a marker of its standing among the great powers, almost independent of whether any specific colony was profitable.

These justifications did not always sit together comfortably, and contemporaries noticed the tension. A colony defended as a civilizing duty was, in the same breath, often defended as a source of raw materials, cheap labor, and captive markets for manufactured goods, an economic dimension the civilizing mission conveniently left in the background. Next lesson takes up exactly this tension, through a British economist who argued the moral language of empire concealed a narrower and more calculating economic motive underneath it.

💡 A CLOSER LOOK AT THE HISTORY
Kipling's poem contains language, describing colonized peoples as "half-devil and half-child," that reflects the racial hierarchy widely, though not universally, assumed among educated Europeans and Americans of his era. This lesson and this week's primary source ask you to read that language as historical evidence of how empire justified itself to itself, not as a view this course treats as accurate or endorses in any way.

Kipling was a British writer born in Bombay, British India, whose fiction and poetry frequently drew on his experience of colonial life. He won the Nobel Prize in Literature in 1907, the first English-language writer to do so, though his reputation today is complicated by the imperial and racial attitudes visible in works like The White Man's Burden.
📚 PART 3 · A POEM ARGUED OVER FROM THE START

Part of what makes Kipling's poem such useful historical evidence is that Americans in 1899 did not read it with one voice. Supporters of retaining the Philippines quoted it approvingly as a serious statement of national responsibility. Critics of the policy, including a vocal American Anti-Imperialist League that counted prominent writers and politicians among its members, mocked the poem or wrote parody versions turning its language back against American expansion, arguing that a republic founded on consent of the governed had no business ruling a colonized people who had not consented to it.

That immediate, divided reaction is itself evidence worth taking seriously. If the civilizing-mission argument had been obviously sincere and uncontroversial to contemporaries, it would not have needed defending in verse, and it would not have provoked such immediate parody from critics who saw through it, or thought they did. The argument over what Kipling's poem actually proved began the week it was published, and this course's own argument over it, in this week's Critical Questions, continues that same contest.

💪 A DUTY CLAIMED IS A DUTY OWED HONESTLY

If a nation claims that ruling another people is a burden shouldered for their benefit, responsibility requires it to actually govern in a way that bears that claim out, not merely to repeat the claim. Whether nineteenth-century empires lived up to the responsibility their own justifications described is exactly the question the rest of this module puts to the test.
