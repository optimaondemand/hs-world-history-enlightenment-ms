Adam Smith and the Case for Self-Interest
Lesson · Capitalism and Its Critics, Part I of II
💡 GLOSSARY OF SUBJECT VOCABULARY
A few words worth reviewing before you read.
capitalism: an economic system in which private individuals, not the state, own capital and businesses, and production is guided mainly by market exchange.
division of labor: breaking a production process into separate, specialized tasks performed by different workers.
self-interest: a person's own advantage or benefit, which Smith argues is a more reliable motive than appeals to others' generosity.
free market: an economic system in which prices and production are determined mainly by voluntary exchange rather than government control.
📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
Is history driven by the free exchange of self-interest, or the struggle between classes?
As factories, steam engines, and steel transformed how goods were made, a Scottish philosopher offered one of the most influential explanations ever written for why a market economy, left largely to its own devices, tends to produce prosperity. This lesson asks what Adam Smith actually argued, which is narrower and more careful than the slogans later attached to his name.

From Moral Philosophy to Political Economy

Adam Smith spent most of his career not as an economist, a word barely in use during his lifetime, but as a professor of moral philosophy at the University of Glasgow. His first major book, The Theory of Moral Sentiments (1759), explored how humans develop moral judgment through sympathy for others. Only later, after resigning his professorship and traveling through Europe as a tutor, did Smith turn his full attention to economic questions, publishing An Inquiry into the Nature and Causes of the Wealth of Nations in 1776, the same year as the American Declaration of Independence.

Smith's central observation in The Wealth of Nations is disarmingly simple. People do not generally get their dinner because a butcher, a brewer, and a baker feel generous toward them. They get their dinner because each tradesperson is pursuing their own advantage, and trade lets both sides get something they want. Smith called this appeal to another person's self-love, rather than their charity, the ordinary and reliable engine of nearly all economic exchange, something no complicated theory was needed to see, only honest observation of how people actually behave.

This was not, in Smith's hands, a celebration of greed. He had spent his earlier career studying sympathy and moral sentiment precisely because he believed humans were capable of genuine concern for others. His point in The Wealth of Nations was narrower: that a whole economy of strangers, most of whom will never meet, cannot realistically run on universal benevolence, but can run remarkably well on the more modest and more common motive of ordinary self-interest, properly channeled through voluntary exchange.

📚 PART 2 · PINS, AND THE POWER OF SPECIALIZATION

Smith's second major idea, the division of labor, used an almost comically small example to make a large point. Smith described a pin factory where, if one worker tried to perform every step of making a pin alone, drawing the wire, cutting it, sharpening the point, attaching the head, they might produce only a handful of pins in a day. But if that same work were divided among several workers, each specializing in a single step, the same factory could produce many thousands of pins per worker per day, an almost unbelievable multiplication of output from the very same hands and the very same raw material.

Smith argued this specialization, repeated across an entire economy, was the real engine behind a nation's growing wealth, far more than any stockpile of gold or silver, which many earlier economic theorists had mistakenly treated as the true measure of national wealth. A nation grew rich, in Smith's account, by producing more goods and services efficiently, made possible by workers specializing in narrow tasks and then freely trading the fruits of that specialization with one another.

Smith did not argue that markets should operate with no rules or oversight whatsoever; he supported public education, infrastructure the market would not build on its own, and government action against monopolies that blocked fair competition. His argument was for a general presumption in favor of free exchange, not the total absence of any government role, a distinction later popularizers of Smith's ideas have not always preserved carefully.

💡 A CLOSER LOOK AT THE HISTORY
Smith is often remembered chiefly for the phrase “invisible hand,” which appears only once in the entire, lengthy Wealth of Nations, in a much narrower context than its later fame suggests. Historians of economic thought caution against treating Smith as a simple cheerleader for unregulated markets; his actual writing is more careful, more morally serious, and considerably more qualified than the single famous phrase now attached to his name.
HISTORICAL FIGURE
Adam Smith
AD‑um SMITH · 1723–1790
Smith was a Scottish moral philosopher whose 1776 book The Wealth of Nations argued that self-interest, channeled through free exchange and specialized labor, drives economic prosperity more reliably than either central planning or appeals to universal generosity. Often called the father of modern economics, Smith saw his economic writing as continuous with, not separate from, his earlier work on moral philosophy.
📚 PART 3 · SMITH'S OWN LIST OF WARNINGS

Smith was also more skeptical of businessmen themselves than his later reputation as capitalism's great defender might suggest. He warned explicitly, elsewhere in The Wealth of Nations, that merchants and manufacturers frequently sought to reduce competition in their own favor, through monopolies, tariffs, and other special privileges that benefited producers at the direct expense of ordinary consumers. Smith trusted the impersonal pressure of open competition far more than he trusted the intentions of any individual capitalist, a distinction later readers invoking his name in defense of big business have not always kept in view.

Keep this caution in mind heading into next lesson. Marx and Engels will argue that Smith's whole framework, however carefully qualified, still failed to reckon honestly with the structural imbalance of power between factory owners and factory workers. Whether that criticism lands as a genuine correction to Smith, or as a different question entirely from the one Smith was actually trying to answer, is exactly what this week's Critical Questions will ask you to work through.

💪 RESPONSIBLE SELF-INTEREST STILL ANSWERS TO OTHERS

Smith's own moral philosophy, easy to forget once his economics became famous, insisted that self-interest operates responsibly only inside a framework of law, custom, and genuine sympathy for others, not in a vacuum. This quarter's virtue asks the same question Smith himself never stopped asking: pursuing your own advantage is not automatically irresponsible, but it is not automatically responsible either. The difference lies in what surrounds it.
