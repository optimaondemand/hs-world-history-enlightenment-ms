💡 GLOSSARY OF SUBJECT VOCABULARY
A few words worth reviewing before you read.
bourgeoisie (boor‑zhwah‑ZEE): in Marx's usage, the class that owns factories, capital, and the means of production.
proletariat (proh‑luh‑TAIR‑ee‑ut): in Marx's usage, the working class, who own no capital and sell their labor to survive.
class struggle: Marx and Engels' theory that history's real driving conflict is between social classes with opposing economic interests.
revolution (in this context): a fundamental overthrow of an existing economic and political system, not merely a change of ruler.
📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
Is history driven by the free exchange of self-interest, or the struggle between classes?
Smith wrote before industrial factories reshaped European cities. By the 1840s, two German thinkers looked at those same factories and saw not free exchange benefiting everyone, but a new and brutal form of the oldest conflict in human history: one group controlling the means of production, and another group forced to sell its labor to survive.

An Unlikely Collaboration

Karl Marx was born in 1818 in the German city of Trier, trained as a philosopher, and spent much of his early career as a journalist and political exile, expelled from Germany, France, and Belgium in turn for his radical writing before eventually settling permanently in London. Friedrich Engels, by contrast, came from wealth: his family owned textile factories, including one in Manchester, England, that Engels himself managed for years, giving him direct, firsthand observation of industrial working conditions that shaped his own early book, The Condition of the Working Class in England (1845).

Marx and Engels met in 1844 and began a lifelong intellectual partnership. In 1847, an international organization of workers called the Communist League commissioned them to write a public statement of the movement's principles. Marx and Engels produced the Manifesto of the Communist Party, published in London in February 1848, just weeks before a wave of popular revolutions and uprisings, largely unconnected to their pamphlet at the time, swept across France, the German states, Austria, and Italy.

The Manifesto's famous opening line, warning that “a spectre is haunting Europe, the spectre of Communism,” was not simple exaggeration. European governments in 1848 genuinely feared organized working-class movements, and Marx and Engels wrote their pamphlet explicitly to give that movement a clear, public statement of its own goals rather than leave it to be defined only by its opponents' fears.

📚 PART 2 · ALL HISTORY AS THE STORY OF CLASS CONFLICT

The Manifesto's central historical claim opens Chapter I directly: that the history of every society that has existed is, at bottom, a history of class struggle, conflict between an oppressing and an oppressed class. Marx and Engels traced this pattern through freeman and slave in the ancient world, lord and serf in the feudal Middle Ages, and, in their own industrial age, a newly simplified conflict between just two great opposing classes: the bourgeoisie, who own factories and capital, and the proletariat, industrial workers who own no capital and must sell their labor to survive.

Where Smith saw individual buyers and sellers freely exchanging goods to their mutual advantage, Marx and Engels saw something structurally different: a relationship where the bourgeoisie's ownership of factories and machinery gave it decisive power over proletarian workers who had no realistic alternative but to accept whatever wages and conditions factory owners offered. This was not, in their account, a matter of individual employers being cruel or kind. It was a built-in feature of the entire economic system, regardless of any particular factory owner's personal character.

Marx and Engels predicted this conflict would eventually sharpen rather than resolve peacefully, as the proletariat grew larger and more organized, until it overthrew the bourgeoisie entirely and reorganized society on a fundamentally different, classless basis. The Manifesto's famous closing call, urging “workingmen of all countries” to unite, treated this coming conflict not as a distant hope but as an approaching historical necessity, one Marx and Engels believed their own analysis had scientifically identified rather than merely wished for.

💡 A CLOSER LOOK AT THE HISTORY
The 1848 revolutions that broke out across Europe just weeks after the Manifesto's publication were not, in fact, caused by it; most participants in those uprisings had never read it, and the revolutions themselves grew out of local grievances, food shortages, and nationalist movements largely separate from Marx and Engels' specific theory. The Manifesto's own influence grew enormously in the decades that followed, but crediting it directly with sparking 1848 confuses coincidence in timing with actual historical cause, exactly the kind of careless causal claim this course has asked you to watch for all year.

KARL MARKS · 1818–1883
Marx was a German philosopher and journalist, repeatedly exiled for his radical writing, who co-authored the Communist Manifesto with Friedrich Engels in 1848 and later wrote the multi-volume economic study Capital. His theory that class struggle drives historical change became the foundation for socialist and communist movements worldwide in the century after his death.
📚 PART 3 · AN IDEA THAT OUTLIVED ITS AUTHORS BY A CENTURY

Marx died in 1883, decades before his ideas reshaped the twentieth century far more dramatically than either he or Engels likely imagined in 1848. Revolutionary movements invoking Marx's name would go on to seize power in Russia in 1917, in China in 1949, and in numerous other nations across the following decades, producing governments and outcomes that varied enormously from one another and, in crucial respects, from what Marx himself had specifically predicted. Later this course will study some of those movements directly. For now, hold the distinction carefully: the argument you are studying this week is the 1848 pamphlet itself, not everything that was later done, sometimes faithfully and sometimes not, in its name.

💪 RESPONSIBILITY FOR THE SYSTEM YOU BENEFIT FROM

Whether or not you find Marx and Engels' full theory convincing, their challenge is worth taking seriously on its own terms: it is not enough to ask whether individual people are behaving kindly within an economic system. Responsibility, this quarter's virtue, also asks whether the system itself distributes power and hardship fairly, a structural question individual kindness alone cannot answer.
