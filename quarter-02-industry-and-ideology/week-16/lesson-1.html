9.3
The Berlin Conference and the Partition of Africa
Lesson · The Scramble, Part I of II
💡 GLOSSARY OF SUBJECT VOCABULARY
A few words worth reviewing before you read.
partition: the division of a territory into separate parts, typically by an outside power or powers.
sphere of influence: a territory or region in which an outside power claims special economic or political privileges, without necessarily governing it directly.
plenipotentiary: a diplomat given full authority to negotiate and sign agreements on behalf of their government.
effective occupation: the principle, established at the Berlin Conference, that a European power's claim to African territory required actually establishing administrative authority there, not merely announcing the claim.
📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
Who drew the lines across the map, and by what right?
In 1884 and 1885, representatives of fourteen countries met in Berlin to agree on rules for dividing African territory among themselves. Not a single African ruler or representative was invited. This lesson asks what the conference actually decided, and what its own document reveals about the claims European powers made to justify deciding it at all.

the Congo Basin: the vast river basin in central Africa whose "free trade" status was a central topic of the Berlin Conference, and which became the personal colonial possession of Belgium's King Leopold II under conference rulings.
Berlin: the capital of the newly unified German Empire, chosen to host the 1884–85 conference partly to showcase Germany's new standing among the great powers.
📚 PART 1 · FOURTEEN NATIONS, ONE CONTINENT, NO AFRICANS

Why Berlin, Why 1884

By the early 1880s, European powers were rapidly staking overlapping claims to African territory, especially around the Congo River basin, where Belgium's King Leopold II had established a private colonial enterprise and other powers worried about being shut out of its trade. German Chancellor Otto von Bismarck, whose Module 8 policy of iron and blood had already reshaped Europe, invited representatives of the major powers to Berlin to negotiate rules for this expansion before rival claims produced open conflict among Europeans themselves.

Fourteen nations sent delegates: the major European powers, plus smaller states like Denmark and Sweden-Norway, plus the United States and the Ottoman Empire. Not a single African kingdom, state, or people was invited to participate or consulted about the outcome, despite the conference's entire purpose being the disposition of their territory. The resulting document, the General Act of the Berlin Conference, was signed in February 1885.

The General Act's own preamble, quoted in this week's primary source, describes its purpose using language of trade, civilization, and the "moral and material well-being" of African populations. Read that language carefully once you reach the primary source, and ask what work it is doing in a document written entirely by outside powers.

📚 PART 2 · EFFECTIVE OCCUPATION AND THE RACE THAT FOLLOWED

The Berlin Conference's most consequential rule was not about any specific territory, but about the general procedure for claiming new territory. Articles 34 and 35 of the General Act, examined closely in this week's primary source, established the principle of effective occupation: a European power could not simply announce a claim to African territory, it had to demonstrate actual administrative authority there, and notify the other signatory powers so they could contest the claim if they wished.

This rule, intended to prevent conflict among European powers, had the effect of accelerating the partition it was meant to regulate. Because a merely announced claim no longer sufficed, powers rushed to establish real administrative and military presence across as much African territory as possible, racing rivals to the ground rather than merely to the negotiating table. Within three decades of the conference, European powers controlled nearly the entire continent, with only Ethiopia, which decisively defeated an Italian invasion force at the Battle of Adwa in 1896, and Liberia remaining independent.

The borders these powers drew rarely followed existing African political, ethnic, or linguistic boundaries, often splitting unified peoples across separate colonies or forcing together groups with little shared history under a single colonial administration. Many of Africa's present-day national borders still trace these lines, a lasting consequence of decisions made entirely without African participation.

💡 A CLOSER LOOK AT THE HISTORY
The partition of Africa is a useful test of this course's contingency-first approach. The specific borders that emerged were not predetermined by geography, ethnicity, or any deep historical logic; they reflected the particular diplomatic bargains, rivalries, and administrative conveniences of the powers meeting in Berlin and afterward. A different balance of power in 1884, or a different set of personalities at the negotiating table, could plausibly have produced a substantially different map.
HISTORICAL FIGURE
Leopold II of Belgium

Leopold II was king of Belgium from 1865 to 1909, but the territory recognized at the Berlin Conference as the Congo Free State was his personal property, not Belgium's, administered through a brutal forced-labor system extracting rubber and ivory that caused enormous suffering and loss of life among the Congolese population, eventually forcing Leopold to cede control to the Belgian state itself in 1908 under international pressure.
📚 PART 3 · A CONFERENCE WITHOUT A LASTING TREATY OF ITS OWN

It is worth noting what the Berlin Conference did not do. It did not itself assign specific African territories to specific European powers; those assignments came later, through separate bilateral treaties, wars, and administrative decisions made country by country, often violently, over the following three decades. What the conference actually provided was a shared procedural framework, the rules of the race, rather than the race's actual results.

This distinction matters for how you read this week's primary source. The General Act's language about trade and the well-being of native populations was not empty in the sense of accomplishing nothing; it genuinely shaped how the partition proceeded, on European terms, according to European procedures. But it was also not a document that gave African peoples any say in the process it set in motion, a gap its own moral language never closed.

💪 DECIDING FOR OTHERS DEMANDS ANSWERING TO THEM

The Berlin Conference's deepest failure of responsibility was not any specific border it drew, but its basic structure: fourteen outside powers made decisions binding on peoples who had no voice in making them and no recourse against them. Responsible use of power requires being answerable to those it affects. A process built to exclude that answerability from the start cannot make up for the exclusion afterward.
