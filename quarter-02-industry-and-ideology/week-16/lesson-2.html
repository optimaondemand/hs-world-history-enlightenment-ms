💡 GLOSSARY OF SUBJECT VOCABULARY
A few words worth reviewing before you read.
imperial commissioner: a high-ranking official appointed directly by the Chinese emperor with special authority to handle a specific crisis.
indemnity: a payment demanded as compensation, often imposed on a defeated power after a war.
extraterritoriality: a legal arrangement in which a foreign power's citizens are subject to their own nation's laws rather than the host country's laws while on its territory.
Boxer Rebellion: an anti-foreign, anti-Christian uprising in China (1899–1901) that was ultimately crushed by an international coalition of foreign powers.
📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
Who drew the lines across the map, and by what right?
While European powers partitioned Africa outright, China faced a different but related form of pressure: Western powers repeatedly forcing open Chinese ports and markets by military force, without ever fully colonizing Chinese territory the way they colonized Africa. This lesson follows one Chinese official's direct appeal against that pressure, and the larger pattern of resistance and defeat that followed it across the rest of the century.

The Opium Crisis at Canton

By the late 1830s, British merchants were importing enormous quantities of opium into China, grown in British India and sold illegally to Chinese buyers despite a standing Chinese ban on the drug. The trade had grown so large that it was draining Chinese silver reserves and causing widespread addiction, alarming the imperial court in Beijing. In 1839, the Qing government appointed Lin Zexu, an experienced and respected official, as imperial commissioner with special authority to end the opium trade at the southern port city of Canton.

Lin acted decisively, confiscating and destroying more than a thousand tons of British-owned opium and pressuring foreign merchants to sign pledges never to trade in it again. Before taking the most severe measures available to him, however, Lin composed a formal letter addressed directly to Britain's young Queen Victoria, appealing to what he described as shared moral principles between nations and asking the British government to voluntarily halt the opium trade its own merchants were conducting.

This week's primary source presents Lin's letter in his own translated words. Notice how he frames the argument: not as a demand backed by threat, but as an appeal to a moral logic he expected Queen Victoria's government to already share.

📚 PART 2 · FROM APPEAL TO DEFEAT, REPEATED

There is no evidence Lin's letter ever reached Queen Victoria directly, and Britain's actual response was not moral reconsideration but war. The First Opium War (1839–1842) ended in decisive British victory, forcing China to sign the Treaty of Nanjing, which ceded Hong Kong to Britain, opened five ports to foreign trade, and granted British subjects extraterritoriality, exemption from Chinese law while on Chinese soil. A Second Opium War (1856–1860), fought jointly by Britain and France, extracted further concessions, including the legalization of the opium trade itself.

This pattern, foreign military pressure forcing new concessions from an unwilling but militarily outmatched Chinese government, repeated across the rest of the century. By 1900, foreign powers had carved China into overlapping "spheres of influence," each claiming special trading and railroad privileges in specific regions, without formally colonizing Chinese territory the way European powers colonized Africa. Anger over this pressure, and over Christian missionary activity many Chinese associated with it, erupted in 1899 into the Boxer Rebellion, an uprising that besieged foreign legations in Beijing before being crushed by a coalition of eight foreign powers, who then imposed a further punishing indemnity on the Chinese government.

China's experience differs instructively from Africa's. Rather than outright colonial partition, China retained nominal sovereignty and its own government throughout this period, but repeatedly lost the practical ability to set its own trade policy, control foreign residents on its own soil, or resist further demands, a form of what historians sometimes call informal empire.

💡 A CLOSER LOOK AT THE HISTORY
China's "informal empire" experience is a useful contrast to Africa's outright partition, and this course resists reducing either to a single simple story. China's continued nominal independence should not be mistaken for genuine sovereignty during this period, and Africa's colonial partition should not obscure the real, organized resistance many African states and peoples mounted, sometimes successfully, against it. Both continents faced the same underlying pressure from industrialized Western power, expressed through different mechanisms shaped by each region's own specific circumstances.

Lin Zexu was a Qing dynasty official appointed imperial commissioner in 1839 to suppress the illegal opium trade at Canton. His confiscation of British-owned opium and his direct appeal to Queen Victoria preceded the First Opium War, whose defeat led to his own political disgrace and exile, though he is remembered in China today as a symbol of principled resistance to foreign pressure.
📚 PART 3 · TWO CONTINENTS, ONE PRESSURE

Set beside Lesson 9.3, China's experience clarifies what made Africa's partition possible in the first place. Both continents faced the same underlying industrial and military imbalance with Western powers, but China's existing centralized government, however outmatched militarily, gave it a structure that could negotiate, however unequally, rather than simply be divided up wholesale the way many African societies without comparable centralized states were.

That comparison should not be read as ranking one region's suffering against the other's. It is meant to sharpen this week's question: the same underlying imbalance of industrial and military power produced different specific outcomes, outright colonial partition in one region, treaty ports and spheres of influence in the other, depending on the existing political structures each outside power actually encountered.

💪 POWER OWES AN ANSWER, EVEN WHEN IT ISN'T ASKED FOR ONE

Lin Zexu's letter tested whether a genuinely responsible use of British commercial power required answering his moral appeal honestly, rather than simply relying on superior military force to settle the disagreement. The verdict of the following decades, repeated wars and repeated concessions, suggests that appeal without matching power rarely succeeds against an unwilling party. Whether that fact excuses the party holding the power is a separate question this week's discussion will ask you to weigh directly.
