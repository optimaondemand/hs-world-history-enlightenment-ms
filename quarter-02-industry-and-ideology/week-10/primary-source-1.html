📖 BEFORE YOU READ

This excerpt comes from Chapter V, “Of Tillage,” where Tull lays out his central theory in his own words. Spelling and capitalization follow the original 1731 edition; minor OCR scanning artifacts from the digitized copy have been silently corrected for readability, with no wording changed.

As you read, notice that Tull is not simply describing a tool. He is making a scientific argument, even if it turned out to be only partly correct.

📚 FROM THE HORSE-HOEING HUSBANDRY, CHAPTER V, OF TILLAGE

“Tillage is breaking and dividing the ground by spade, plough, hoe, or other instruments, which divide by a sort of attrition (or contusion) as dung does by fermentation… since the artificial pasture of plants is made and increased by pulveration, 'tis no matter whether it be by the ferment of dung, the attrition of the plough, the contusion of the roller, or by any other instrument or means whatsoever.”

Jethro Tull, The Horse-Hoeing Husbandry (1731), Ch. V

“By dung we are limited to the quantity of it we can procure, which in most places is too scanty: but by tillage, we can enlarge our field of subterranean pasture without limitation, tho' the external surface of it be confined within narrow bounds.”

Jethro Tull, The Horse-Hoeing Husbandry (1731), Ch. V

“Every time the earth is broken by any sort of tillage, or division, there must arise some new superficies of the broken parts, which never has been open before.”

Jethro Tull, The Horse-Hoeing Husbandry (1731), Ch. V
💡 WORDS YOU MIGHT NOT KNOW
attrition / contusion: wearing down or bruising by rubbing or striking, here describing how a plough or hoe breaks soil apart.
pulveration: the act of grinding or reducing something to fine particles, here referring to how tillage breaks up soil.
superficies: a surface, here referring to the newly exposed surface area of soil particles after tillage.
subterranean pasture: Tull's term for the soil itself, imagined as feeding ground for plant roots the way a field feeds grazing animals.
